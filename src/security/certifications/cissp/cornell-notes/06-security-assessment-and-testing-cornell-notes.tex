\section*{Domain Overview}
Security assessment and testing turns control claims into evidence. The domain spans strategy, technical testing, process measurements, reporting/remediation, exception handling, disclosure, and formal audits. The central question is not whether a control exists on paper but whether it is implemented, operating as intended, and producing the required risk-reduction outcome.

\begin{summarybox}{Review Objectives}
Design internal/external/third-party evaluation strategies; distinguish vulnerability assessment from penetration testing; use technical and process testing methods; create meaningful KPIs/KRIs; analyze and report findings; manage remediation/exceptions/disclosure; and conduct or facilitate internal, external, and supplier audits.
\end{summarybox}

\section{Assessment, Test, and Audit Strategy}
\source{pp. 420--423}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Assessment vs. test vs. audit}{All seek evidence about security, but scope and formality differ. Testing exercises a control/system; assessment evaluates effectiveness against criteria; audit is a more formal, independent examination against defined requirements. Define objective and assurance level before choosing method.}
\noterow{Internal evaluation}{Performed by organizational personnel who know the environment and can test frequently at lower cost. Familiarity aids context and remediation but can reduce independence or allow blind spots/conflicts of interest.}
\noterow{External evaluation}{Independent specialists provide objectivity and skills and may be required by regulation/contract. They cost more and need time to learn the environment; scope, confidentiality, evidence access, and communication must be governed.}
\noterow{Third-party evidence}{Assessments/reports from suppliers or independent assessors can provide assurance about outsourced services. Confirm scope, period, standards, exceptions/findings, subservice dependencies, and whether the evidence actually covers your use case.}
\noterow{Testing cadence}{Combine periodic testing with continuous monitoring and event-driven reassessment after major changes, new threats, incidents, or control failures. Point-in-time evidence becomes stale as systems and risk change.}
\end{cornell}

\section{Security Control Testing}
\source{pp. 423--441}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Vulnerability assessment}{Systematically identify and prioritize weaknesses without necessarily exploiting them. Use authenticated/unauthenticated scans, configuration checks, discovery, and manual validation; correlate findings with asset value/exposure and compensating controls.}
\noterow{Scanner limitations}{Expect false positives, false negatives, credential/permission problems, unstable targets, scan traffic impacts, outdated signatures, and incomplete asset coverage. Validate significant findings and tune scope/configuration rather than treating a scan report as ground truth.}
\noterow{Vulnerability vs. penetration test}{A vulnerability assessment primarily identifies/reports potential weaknesses. A penetration test attempts authorized exploitation to show whether weaknesses can actually lead to access/impact. Pen testing gives stronger exploitability evidence but carries greater cost, legal, and operational risk.}
\noterow{White-box testing}{Tester receives extensive internal information. Increases coverage/efficiency and can simulate insider knowledge but does not test all concealment/reconnaissance defenses.}
\noterow{Black-box testing}{Tester begins with little/no internal knowledge, approximating an external attacker and testing discovery defenses. Limited time can leave unknown assets untested, so absence of findings is not proof of absence.}
\noterow{Gray-box testing}{Tester receives selected knowledge/credentials while still performing discovery. Balances efficient deep coverage with some attacker-like reconnaissance.}
\noterow{Rules of engagement}{Obtain written authorization and define in/out-of-scope targets, allowed/forbidden techniques, test accounts/access, physical/social-engineering scope, timing, tester identities/tools, safety/stop conditions, sensitive-data handling/destruction, communication/escalation, retesting, and report expectations.}
\noterow{Pen-test Phase 1 -- Reconnaissance}{Gather target information, often via OSINT, DNS/public records, social media, attack-surface services, and physical observation. The goal is to understand targets with minimal detection.}
\noterow{Phase 2 -- Scanning/probing}{Enumerate hosts/services, ports, versions/banners, endpoints, and vulnerabilities. Tools can automate footprinting and prioritize likely entry points; active probing increases detection and disruption risk.}
\noterow{Phase 3 -- Exploitation}{Use only approved methods to exploit weaknesses and obtain unauthorized-equivalent access. Closely monitor impact; proof should be sufficient to demonstrate risk without unnecessary damage.}
\noterow{Phase 4 -- Post-exploitation}{Use gained access to enumerate internal resources, escalate privilege, pivot, and assess what a real attacker could reach, within engagement limits. This demonstrates attack paths and blast radius.}
\noterow{Phase 5 -- Reporting / retest}{Document actions, evidence, findings, impact, and recommended remediation for technical and executive audiences. After remediation, retest relevant issues to verify that the fix closes the exploit path without creating new problems.}
\noterow{Physical penetration testing}{Tests doors, guards, badges, visitor processes, social engineering, and facility controls. Because testers may appear to trespass, authorization, safety, guard/law-enforcement coordination, and an escalation/contact mechanism are essential.}
\noterow{Log review}{Examine security/system/application/network records for control operation, anomalies, unauthorized changes, and accountability. Protect log integrity, synchronize time, retain according to need, centralize where practical, and ensure someone actually reviews/alerts on meaningful events.}
\noterow{Synthetic transactions}{Automated known transactions run against a service to verify availability and expected behavior from the user/system perspective. Useful for continuously testing critical workflows rather than only infrastructure health.}
\noterow{Code review and testing}{Static/manual review and automated code analysis can find security defects before runtime; dynamic testing exercises running behavior. Combine techniques because no one method finds every flaw and false results require interpretation.}
\noterow{Misuse-case / negative testing}{Exercise unexpected, invalid, abusive, boundary, or malicious inputs and sequences rather than only expected use cases. Verify the system rejects unsafe actions and fails securely.}
\noterow{Test coverage analysis}{Measure how much of the target's functions, requirements, code paths, controls, or interfaces are exercised. High percentage is useful only if the tested cases are meaningful; untested critical paths can dominate residual risk.}
\noterow{Interface testing}{Test UIs, APIs, service boundaries, file/data exchanges, and inter-system integration for authentication, authorization, input validation, error handling, rate/sequence behavior, and trust assumptions. Interfaces are natural attack surfaces.}
\noterow{Breach and Attack Simulation (BAS)}{Automates recurring attacker-like techniques to validate whether preventive/detective controls see and stop realistic behaviors. BAS complements rather than replaces human penetration testing and requires safe scenarios and meaningful outcome measurement.}
\noterow{Compliance checks}{Compare implemented controls/evidence against explicit framework, regulatory, contractual, or internal requirements. Compliance testing answers conformance questions; it does not prove that all real-world security risk is adequately managed.}
\end{cornell}

\begin{exambox}{Testing Safety}
Authorization and scope precede exploitation. A penetration tester is permitted to emulate an attacker only within documented rules. A technically valid test outside authorization can become an incident or legal violation.
\end{exambox}

\section{Collect Security Process Data}
\source{pp. 442--451}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Technical control/process data}{Collect configuration state, vulnerability/patch status, endpoint/network telemetry, security alerts, change records, scan results, and control-test outcomes. Normalize and trend data so it answers control/risk questions instead of becoming an inventory of unrelated numbers.}
\noterow{Administrative controls}{Test policies, procedures, approvals, exceptions, risk records, training evidence, vendor controls, and documented governance. Evidence should show that the process is followed, not merely that a document exists.}
\noterow{Account management evidence}{Measure joiner/mover/leaver timeliness, stale/orphaned/privileged/service accounts, access reviews, failed/abnormal logins, role conflicts, and deprovisioning. IAM evidence is a strong indicator of least privilege and accountability.}
\noterow{Management review and approval}{High-risk decisions, changes, access, exceptions, and remediation often require accountable approval. Verify approvals are performed by authorized people, are timely, contain required evidence, and are not simply rubber stamps.}
\noterow{Compliance management reviews}{Leadership should receive evidence of conformance, deficiencies, owners, due dates, and residual risk. Recurring review supports corrective action and demonstrates governance accountability.}
\noterow{Metric vs. KPI}{A \textbf{metric} is a measurement. A \textbf{KPI} ties selected measurements to progress toward a performance objective. Good KPIs are relevant, consistently defined, actionable, and trended; raw counts without context can reward the wrong behavior.}
\noterow{Key Risk Indicator (KRI)}{Forward-looking or exposure-focused signal that risk may be increasing, such as vulnerability age, unsupported assets, concentration, control failures, or threat activity. Thresholds should trigger defined escalation or treatment rather than passive reporting.}
\noterow{Backup verification}{A successful backup job does not prove recoverability. Monitor job completion/errors and periodically restore representative data/systems, validate integrity/usability, measure recovery objectives, and address missing/corrupt/obsolete media before an incident.}
\noterow{Training and awareness data}{Measure completion plus knowledge and behavior outcomes: assessment scores, phishing/simulation results, reporting behavior, incidents, policy violations, and role-specific capability. Use results to adjust content and delivery.}
\noterow{DR/BC evidence}{Review plan currency, dependency/asset changes, exercise results, recovery times, communications, alternate resources, lessons learned, and remediation. Test whether business functions can actually meet their recovery requirements.}
\end{cornell}

\section{Analyze Output, Report, and Remediate}
\source{pp. 452--457}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Analysis principle}{Correlate evidence, validate findings, remove duplicates, understand root cause/attack path, consider asset criticality and compensating controls, assign severity/priority, and communicate uncertainty. A scanner's severity alone is not the business-risk decision.}
\noterow{Typical audit report}{Common elements include purpose/objective, scope and criteria, methodology/period, executive summary, findings/evidence, risk/impact, recommendations or management response, and limitations. Technical detail can be separated from executive decision information.}
\noterow{Remediation plan}{For each finding document the issue, context/mitigating factors, priority, accountable owner, resources, milestones, target date, and expected outcome. Frameworks may call this a POA\&M or similar action plan.}
\noterow{Retesting}{Closing a ticket is not verification. Re-run the relevant test or otherwise obtain evidence that remediation worked and did not merely hide the symptom. Track recurrence/root cause when the same deficiency returns.}
\noterow{Exception handling}{If a requirement cannot be met, document the risk, reason, compensating controls, explicit management risk acceptance, owner, and \textbf{temporary} expiration/review date. Repeated/permanent exceptions may indicate misaligned policy or unresolved governance.}
\noterow{Ethical/responsible disclosure}{Organizations should publish a clear vulnerability-reporting channel/policy, scope expectations, response timeline, safe-harbor/legal considerations, and recognition/bug-bounty terms where applicable. Researchers should act in good faith, follow scope/law, and give reasonable remediation opportunity.}
\end{cornell}

\section{Conduct or Facilitate Security Audits}
\source{pp. 458--461}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Design an audit program}{Define objectives, applicable standards/criteria, independence, qualified resources, scope/population/sampling, evidence methods, schedule, reporting/escalation, remediation tracking, and follow-up. A recurring program reveals trends beyond one point-in-time audit.}
\noterow{Audit sampling}{Test a representative subset when examining an entire population is impractical. Sampling saves effort but introduces sampling risk; choose a method/size that reflects meaningful subpopulations and the required assurance.}
\noterow{Internal audit}{Organization staff provide context, lower recurring cost, and ongoing remediation support, but may lack independence or overlook familiar problems. Organizational separation and direct governance reporting can improve objectivity.}
\noterow{External audit}{Independent outsiders add objectivity/specialized skills and may satisfy regulatory/certification requirements. Costs and learning curve are higher; facilitate evidence access while protecting sensitive information and preserving audit independence.}
\noterow{Third-party audit}{Evaluate suppliers/processors and inherited risk. Review standards/scope, actual controls, exceptions/findings, remediation, subservice/fourth-party dependencies, and whether the report period/use case matches your reliance.}
\noterow{Framework examples}{The chapter references SSAE 18/SOC 2, Common Criteria-related ISO/IEC evaluation standards, ISO 27006 for ISMS certification bodies, NIST SP 800-53A, NIST CSF, and FedRAMP assessment approaches. Select criteria based on the assurance question and obligation.}
\end{cornell}

\section{High-Yield Review}
\begin{itemize}
\item Test strategy follows objective, risk, independence needs, and required assurance.
\item Scanning identifies potential weakness; penetration testing attempts exploitation and must have written authorization/ROE.
\item Pen test: reconnaissance, scanning/probing, exploitation, post-exploitation, reporting, then remediation/retest.
\item Metrics become useful when tied to objectives (KPI) or changing exposure (KRI).
\item Backup success must be verified by restore; control claims must be verified by operation/evidence.
\item Findings need owners, milestones, verification; exceptions need explicit temporary risk acceptance.
\item External independence and third-party audit evidence help assurance but never eliminate the consumer organization's own risk decision.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{Answer Key}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Assessment identifies/reports potential weaknesses; pen testing attempts approved exploitation to demonstrate actual access/impact.
\item White = extensive knowledge; black = little/no knowledge; gray = selected knowledge plus discovery.
\item Authorization, scope/exclusions, allowed/forbidden methods, timing, accounts, tester identities/tools, safety/stop criteria, communications, data handling, reports and retest expectations.
\item Reconnaissance, scanning/probing, exploitation, post-exploitation, reporting.
\item They continuously exercise a known business/application workflow and show user-visible performance/function rather than infrastructure health alone.
\item How much of a defined target is exercised; it does not prove that the cases are meaningful or that no defect exists in the covered area.
\item Metric is a measurement; KPI tracks performance against an objective; KRI signals changing/increasing risk.
\item Only a restore verifies the backup is accessible, intact, compatible, and capable of meeting recovery needs.
\item Finding/context, priority, owner, resources, milestones/target dates, and expected resolution, followed by verification.
\item Risk/issue, reason, compensating controls, approving authority/risk acceptance, owner, and temporary expiration/review.
\item Internal = organizational context; external = independent assessor; third-party = assurance about supplier/processor controls and inherited risk.
\item Compliance evaluates defined requirements; threats, scope gaps, poor requirements, and changing risk can remain.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Cornell Summary}
\begin{summarybox}{Domain 6 Summary}
Assessment and testing provide the evidence loop of risk management. Define what assurance is needed, test controls with methods suited to the target, collect both technical and governance evidence, measure performance and risk, validate findings, assign accountable remediation, retest fixes, and explicitly govern any temporary exceptions. Audits add structured criteria and independence, while penetration testing adds adversarial exploitability evidence. None of these activities is useful unless the results change decisions and improve control effectiveness.
\end{summarybox}
