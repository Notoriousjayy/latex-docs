  {\large Matching, Constraints, and Optimization}\\[0.6em]
  {\small A practical guide to choosing the right algorithmic foundation for workforce rostering}

\vspace{0.4em}

\begin{keybox}
\textbf{Core conclusion.}
Employee scheduling is best modeled primarily as a \emph{constraint-optimization} problem.
Stable matching remains useful for preference-allocation subproblems, but it is rarely sufficient as the central engine for a production workforce scheduler.
\end{keybox}

\section{1. Why Matching Theory Still Matters}

Knowledge of matching and scheduling algorithms is directly useful when designing an employee scheduler.
The classical Stable Marriage Problem and the Gale--Shapley algorithm are especially valuable for understanding how preferences can be reconciled between two sets of participants.

A scheduling system may be viewed as involving:

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Employees}, who may rank shifts, days, locations, or time windows.
  \item \textbf{Management or shifts}, which may impose preferences or requirements involving skills, seniority, labor cost, coverage, or fairness.
\end{itemize}

Stable matching introduces the useful concept of a \emph{blocking pair}: two participants who would mutually prefer each other over their assigned outcomes. Avoiding blocking pairs can be valuable when preference stability itself is a design objective.

\begin{cautionbox}
\textbf{Important limitation.}
A stable outcome is not automatically a fair, equitable, employee-optimal, cost-optimal, or satisfaction-maximizing outcome.
Stability is a specific mathematical property, not a synonym for overall scheduling quality.
\end{cautionbox}

\section{2. Why Gale--Shapley Is Usually Not the Scheduling Engine}

Classical stable marriage assumes one-to-one matching. Workforce scheduling is considerably more coupled:

\[
\text{Employees}
\times
\text{Shifts}
\times
\text{Days}
\times
\text{Skills}
\times
\text{Hours}
\times
\text{Rules}
\times
\text{Preferences}.
\]

A single assignment can affect many other decisions. Assigning one employee to a Tuesday night shift may also influence:

\begin{itemize}
  \item Wednesday-morning eligibility,
  \item weekly-hour limits,
  \item overtime,
  \item minimum-rest requirements,
  \item weekend rotation,
  \item skill coverage,
  \item staffing cost, and
  \item the feasibility of every remaining assignment.
\end{itemize}

Many-to-one variants such as the Hospitals/Residents problem broaden stable matching beyond one-to-one assignment, but they still do not natively express the full set of temporal, legal, and combinatorial constraints found in workforce rostering.

\section{3. A Better Mathematical Model}

A practical starting point is to define a binary decision variable:

\[
x_{e,s} =
\begin{cases}
1, & \text{if employee } e \text{ is assigned to shift } s,\\
0, & \text{otherwise.}
\end{cases}
\]

The scheduling problem then becomes a search for values of \(x_{e,s}\) that satisfy all mandatory rules while optimizing one or more business objectives.

\subsection{Hard constraints}

Hard constraints define feasibility. Typical examples include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item required staffing coverage,
  \item employee availability,
  \item required skills or certifications,
  \item no overlapping assignments,
  \item maximum weekly hours,
  \item minimum rest periods,
  \item contractual work rules,
  \item labor-law requirements, and
  \item mandatory role or shift composition.
\end{itemize}

A solver can guarantee that these constraints are satisfied \emph{only if it returns a feasible solution and the rules have been modeled correctly}. An over-constrained model may be infeasible.

\subsection{Soft constraints and objectives}

Soft constraints represent preferences or optimization goals. Examples include:

\begin{itemize}
  \item preferred shifts,
  \item requested days off,
  \item balanced weekend assignments,
  \item equitable distribution of undesirable shifts,
  \item seniority preferences,
  \item overtime minimization,
  \item labor-cost minimization,
  \item continuity of assignments, and
  \item minimizing changes to an already-published schedule.
\end{itemize}

A common formulation is a weighted objective:

\[
\min
\left(
w_1 C_{\text{overtime}}
+
w_2 C_{\text{preference}}
+
w_3 C_{\text{fairness}}
+
w_4 C_{\text{schedule-change}}
\right),
\]

where each \(w_i\) expresses the relative importance of an objective.

\begin{keybox}
\[
\boxed{
\text{Employee Scheduling}
=
\text{Feasibility Constraints}
+
\text{Optimization Objectives}
+
\text{Preference Modeling}
}
\]
\end{keybox}

\section{4. Comparing Algorithmic Approaches}

\small
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{>{\bfseries}p{0.22\textwidth} Y Y}
\toprule
Approach & Best role & Important caveat \\
\midrule
Stable Matching / Gale--Shapley
& Preference-based allocation where stability itself matters.
& Does not naturally model complex temporal, legal, or weekly coupling. \\

Hungarian / Assignment Algorithm
& Simple one-worker-to-one-task cost minimization.
& Becomes inadequate once assignments interact across time or capacity. \\

Min-Cost Flow
& Structured assignment and capacity problems.
& Excellent for network-like models, but less expressive for complex logical constraints. \\

MILP / ILP
& Linear constraints with mathematically defined optimization objectives.
& Global optimality is guaranteed only when the model is solved to optimality; production runs may stop earlier with an optimality gap. \\

Constraint Programming / CP-SAT
& Complex logical, temporal, Boolean, and combinatorial scheduling.
& Requires careful modeling, especially around objective weighting and scalability. \\

Local Search / Metaheuristics
& Very large problems where a high-quality solution is more important than proof of optimality.
& Solution quality can depend on tuning and search strategy. \\

Hybrid Optimization
& Enterprise-scale rostering combining exact solving, heuristics, repair, and replanning.
& More engineering complexity, but often the most practical architecture at scale. \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\normalsize

\section{5. Where Stable Matching Fits Best}

Stable matching should remain part of the scheduling toolkit, particularly for relatively self-contained allocation problems such as:

\begin{itemize}
  \item assigning employees to vacation periods,
  \item allocating employees to teams or departments,
  \item assigning rotations or residency-style placements,
  \item allocating preferred work locations,
  \item assigning scarce training opportunities,
  \item mentor--mentee matching.
\end{itemize}

These problems resemble:

\[
\text{Employees} \leftrightarrow \text{Opportunities}
\]

rather than the highly coupled structure of a weekly or monthly roster.

\section{6. Recommended Solver Architecture}

\begin{recommendbox}
\textbf{Default recommendation.}

For a new scheduler, model the problem as a constraint-optimization system.

\begin{itemize}
  \item \textbf{Python, C++, Java, or C\#:} Google OR-Tools CP-SAT is a strong default choice.
  \item \textbf{Java/Kotlin-centric systems:} Timefold Solver is a strong alternative, especially when modeling explicit hard, medium, and soft constraints.
\end{itemize}

Treat availability, coverage, certifications, overlap, hour caps, rest periods, and legal requirements as \textbf{hard constraints}. Treat preferences, fairness, labor cost, overtime, continuity, and convenience as \textbf{soft constraints or weighted objectives}.
\end{recommendbox}

\subsection{Suggested high-level architecture}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Domain model} --- employees, shifts, skills, availability, contracts, preferences, and locations.
  \item \textbf{Constraint layer} --- hard and soft scheduling rules.
  \item \textbf{Optimization engine} --- CP-SAT, MILP, Timefold, or a hybrid search.
  \item \textbf{Scoring/objective layer} --- fairness, cost, preference satisfaction, overtime, continuity.
  \item \textbf{Validation layer} --- independent post-solve checks for legal and business constraints.
  \item \textbf{Explainability layer} --- human-readable reasons for assignments, violations, or compromises.
  \item \textbf{Repair/replanning layer} --- partial re-optimization after call-outs, leave, demand changes, or shift swaps.
\end{enumerate}

\section{7. Production Engineering Considerations}

A production scheduler should be designed around more than raw optimization quality.

\subsection{Feasibility first}
Use hard constraints to ensure that impossible or non-compliant assignments never reach the published roster.

\subsection{Optimization second}
Once feasibility is established, optimize cost, preference satisfaction, fairness, overtime, and schedule stability.

\subsection{Graceful infeasibility}
When no feasible schedule exists, the system should identify which requirements are preventing feasibility rather than simply returning ``no solution.''

\subsection{Incremental replanning}
Real schedules change. A strong system should support partial repair so that a single absence does not unnecessarily rewrite the entire roster.

\subsection{Explainable decisions}
Managers and employees should be able to understand why a shift was assigned, why a preference could not be honored, or which constraint prevented a requested change.

\section{8. Decision Rule}

Use the following rule of thumb:

\begin{center}
\begin{tabularx}{0.94\textwidth}{>{\bfseries}p{0.34\textwidth} Y}
\toprule
Problem shape & Preferred technique \\
\midrule
Simple one-to-one assignment & Assignment algorithm / Hungarian method \\
Preference stability is the main concern & Stable matching \\
Capacity-constrained structured allocation & Min-cost flow \\
Complex linear business optimization & MILP / ILP \\
Complex scheduling with temporal and logical rules & CP-SAT / constraint programming \\
Massive search space with acceptable near-optimality & Local search / metaheuristics \\
Large production workforce platform & Hybrid optimization architecture \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{center}

\section{9. Practical Takeaway}

The strongest conceptual shift is this:

\begin{quote}
Do not ask, ``Which matching algorithm should assign employees to shifts?''

Ask, ``What decision variables, hard constraints, soft constraints, and optimization objectives define a valid and desirable schedule?''
\end{quote}

Once the problem is expressed that way, the correct solver family becomes much easier to select.
