\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.1 Channel allocation problem} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright The MAC problem is to decide which ready station may use a common channel, while balancing utilization, delay, and fairness.\par\begin{itemize}\item \textbf{4.1.1 Static allocation} Frequency-division or another fixed partition gives each of $N$ users a permanent share. It works for steady traffic but wastes idle shares under bursty demand.\item \textbf{4.1.2 Dynamic-allocation assumptions} Classic models assume independent stations, one shared channel, observable transmission outcomes, and either continuous or slotted time. Results depend on whether carrier state can be sensed and whether collisions are detectable.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.2 Multiple-access protocols} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright Contention is simple and responsive at low load; controlled access becomes attractive as collisions dominate.\par\begin{itemize}\item \textbf{4.2.1 ALOHA} Pure ALOHA transmits when ready and retries after collisions; slotted ALOHA restricts starts to slot boundaries, reducing the vulnerable period and improving peak throughput.\item \textbf{4.2.2 Carrier-sense multiple access} Stations listen before sending. Persistent, nonpersistent, and $p$-persistent rules trade delay against collision risk. CSMA/CD aborts a detected collision and uses randomized binary exponential backoff.\item \textbf{4.2.3 Collision-free protocols} Reservation bit maps, token passing, and binary countdown establish a winner before data transmission. They avoid collisions but add coordination overhead.\item \textbf{4.2.4 Limited-contention protocols} Adaptive schemes combine contention at low load with reservation-like structure at high load, often by organizing stations into groups or a contention tree.\item \textbf{4.2.5 Wireless LAN protocols} A transmitter may not hear a competing station at the receiver (hidden terminal), and carrier sensing may suppress a safe transmission (exposed terminal). Collision avoidance and short request/clear exchanges reduce, but do not eliminate, these problems.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.3 Ethernet} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright \begin{itemize}\item \textbf{4.3.1 Classic physical layer} Classic Ethernet used a shared coaxial or hub-repeated medium with constraints chosen so a sender could detect a worst-case collision while transmitting.\item \textbf{4.3.2 Classic MAC protocol} Frames carry destination and source addresses, type/length information, payload with padding when needed, and a CRC. CSMA/CD and binary exponential backoff govern shared-medium access.\item \textbf{4.3.3 Ethernet performance} Efficiency rises with longer frames and falls as propagation time becomes large relative to frame transmission time. The analytical model depends on offered load and contention slots.\item \textbf{4.3.4 Switched Ethernet} A switch gives stations point-to-point links and forwards frames selectively. Full-duplex switch links remove collisions while retaining the Ethernet frame and addressing model.\item \textbf{4.3.5 Fast Ethernet} The 100-Mbit/s evolution preserved compatibility at the MAC interface and adapted physical media and encoding.\item \textbf{4.3.6 Gigabit Ethernet} Gigabit operation largely targets switched full-duplex links, while compatibility provisions preserve the Ethernet service and frame structure.\item \textbf{4.3.7 10-Gigabit Ethernet} Ten-gigabit Ethernet is full duplex and switch-oriented; long-distance optical options extend Ethernet beyond its original LAN medium.\item \textbf{4.3.8 Retrospective} Ethernet succeeded through simplicity, evolutionary compatibility, commodity economics, and switching, not because one original shared-medium design remained unchanged.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.4 Wireless LANs} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright \begin{itemize}\item \textbf{4.4.1 Architecture and stack} IEEE 802.11 stations form basic service sets. Infrastructure networks use access points and a distribution system; ad hoc networks coordinate directly.\item \textbf{4.4.2 Physical layer} The presented variants use spread-spectrum or orthogonal multicarrier techniques across regulated bands, with rates adapting to radio conditions.\item \textbf{4.4.3 MAC protocol} Distributed coordination uses carrier sensing, interframe spaces, random backoff, and positive acknowledgments. Optional RTS/CTS reserves the medium using virtual carrier sensing.\item \textbf{4.4.4 Frame structure} Frames identify type, duration, addresses, sequence control, payload, and checksum. Multiple address fields support wireless stations, access points, and distribution-system forwarding.\item \textbf{4.4.5 Services} Association, reassociation, disassociation, distribution, integration, authentication, deauthentication, privacy, and delivery services coordinate membership and movement.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.5 Broadband wireless} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright \begin{itemize}\item \textbf{4.5.1 Comparison with 802.11 and 3G} The presented IEEE 802.16 design targets metropolitan broadband with scheduled base-station control, differing from local contention-based WLANs and operator cellular systems.\item \textbf{4.5.2 Architecture and stack} Subscriber stations communicate through a base station whose MAC controls uplink and downlink resources and supports service classes.\item \textbf{4.5.3 Physical layer} Wideband radio uses multicarrier techniques, adaptive modulation, and time or frequency duplexing to handle varying channel conditions.\item \textbf{4.5.4 MAC protocol} Connection-oriented scheduling allocates bandwidth and can distinguish service requirements rather than relying on unscheduled contention for all traffic.\item \textbf{4.5.5 Frame structure} Time frames divide downlink and uplink regions and carry maps that tell stations when and how to transmit.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.6 Bluetooth} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright \begin{itemize}\item \textbf{4.6.1 Architecture} A short-range piconet has a central coordinating device and participating devices; multiple piconets can overlap as a scatternet.\item \textbf{4.6.2 Applications} Profiles define interoperable uses such as audio, peripheral, and data exchange behavior above the base radio links.\item \textbf{4.6.3 Protocol stack} Radio and baseband functions support link management, logical link control, service discovery, and application profiles.\item \textbf{4.6.4 Radio layer} Frequency hopping in the unlicensed band limits persistence on an interfered frequency and supports short-range low-power operation.\item \textbf{4.6.5 Link layers} Synchronous connection-oriented links favor reserved, time-sensitive traffic; asynchronous connectionless links favor packet data with retransmission.\item \textbf{4.6.6 Frame structure} Time slots, addressing/control information, payload formats, and error checks depend on link and packet type.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.7 RFID} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright \begin{itemize}\item \textbf{4.7.1 EPC Gen 2 architecture} A reader sends energy and commands to passive tags, which backscatter responses. Many tags may occupy the reader's field.\item \textbf{4.7.2 Physical layer} Reader-to-tag and tag-to-reader signaling use different modulation and encoding suited to powering and detecting passive devices.\item \textbf{4.7.3 Identification layer} A slotted query process inventories tags and uses randomized replies to reduce repeated collisions.\item \textbf{4.7.4 Identification messages} Commands and responses carry inventory parameters, handles, identifiers, checks, and access information needed for tag selection and operation.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.8 Data-link switching} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright \begin{itemize}\item \textbf{4.8.1 Uses of bridges} Bridges join LAN segments, enlarge coverage, isolate some traffic, and connect compatible link technologies while remaining transparent to hosts.\item \textbf{4.8.2 Learning bridges} A bridge learns a source address on the incoming port. It forwards a known destination only toward the learned port, filters local traffic, and floods unknown or group destinations.\item \textbf{4.8.3 Spanning-tree bridges} Redundant physical links create forwarding loops and duplicate frames. Bridges elect a root and disable selected ports so active links form a loop-free spanning tree.\item \textbf{4.8.4 Repeaters through gateways} Repeaters and hubs operate on signals/bits; bridges and switches forward frames; routers forward packets; higher-layer gateways translate application or protocol semantics.\item \textbf{4.8.5 Virtual LANs} A VLAN forms a logical broadcast domain independent of physical port location. IEEE 802.1Q tags carry VLAN identity across trunk links; routing is required between VLANs.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{4.9 Summary} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright MAC protocols arbitrate shared channels, Ethernet evolves through switching and rate changes, wireless links add hidden-terminal and mobility concerns, and bridges create scalable LANs with learning, loop prevention, and logical segmentation.\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\end{longtable}

\begin{legacybox}Shared-coax Ethernet, hubs, early WiMAX, the presented Bluetooth generation, and the EPC Gen 2 treatment are retained as historical or architecture-building context. Switched full-duplex Ethernet does not use collision detection on its point-to-point links.\end{legacybox}

\notesection{Chapter synthesis}

\begin{overviewbox}\textbf{Cause-and-effect chain.} A broadcast medium permits simultaneous attempts; overlapping attempts cause collisions; MAC rules coordinate access; switches replace one large collision domain with point-to-point links; bridge learning reduces needless forwarding; spanning trees suppress loops; VLANs create logical broadcast boundaries.\par
\textbf{Common confusions.} Collision domains and broadcast domains are different; an 802.11 acknowledgment is link-local; an access point is not automatically a router; and a VLAN tag does not itself route between VLANs.\par
\textbf{Derived insight.} Ethernet's stable frame service survived because the access mechanism could change underneath it from shared collision detection to switched full duplex.\end{overviewbox}

\notesection{Retrieval practice}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Why does fixed channel allocation waste capacity under bursty demand?
  \item Compare pure and slotted ALOHA vulnerable periods and peak behavior.
  \item How do persistent and nonpersistent carrier sensing trade delay for collision probability?
  \item Why is collision detection practical on classic wired Ethernet but not assumed for 802.11?
  \item What problem do RTS and CTS address, and what can they not guarantee?
  \item Trace binary exponential backoff after repeated Ethernet collisions.
  \item Why did switched full-duplex operation change Ethernet's collision behavior?
  \item Explain why an 802.11 frame may need multiple addresses.
  \item Contrast contention-based WLAN access with scheduled broadband-wireless access.
  \item How does a passive RFID tag communicate without a conventional transmitter power source?
  \item Trace a learning bridge's actions for unknown, known-remote, and known-local destinations.
  \item Why are both a spanning tree and VLAN-aware forwarding needed in a redundant segmented LAN?
\end{enumerate}
\begin{CornellSummaryBox}{Rapid-review Cornell summary}Shared-media access ranges from random contention to explicit reservation. Wired collision detection, wireless collision avoidance, scheduled broadband access, short-range hopping, and RFID inventory solve different physical problems. Switches learn frame destinations; spanning trees prevent loops; VLANs create logical broadcast domains.\end{CornellSummaryBox}
