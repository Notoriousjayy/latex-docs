\notesection{Chapter overview}
\begin{overviewbox}
\textbf{Learning objectives}\begin{itemize}
  \item Explain framing and the services a data-link layer may provide to the network layer.
  \item Compare error detection with forward error correction using distance and redundancy.
  \item Trace stop-and-wait, Go-Back-N, and selective-repeat operation under loss or damage.
  \item Relate sequence spaces, windows, acknowledgments, timers, and buffering to correctness.
  \item Recognize how packet-over-SONET and ADSL instantiate link-layer concerns.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Key themes}\quad Framing; transparency; error control; flow control; coding; automatic repeat request; pipelining.\par

\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{3.1 Design issues} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright The layer must preserve framing transparency while managing errors and rate mismatch across one link.\par\begin{itemize}\item \textbf{3.1.1 Services to the network layer} Possible services are unacknowledged connectionless, acknowledged connectionless, and acknowledged connection-oriented delivery. The appropriate choice depends on medium error behavior and higher-layer needs.\item \textbf{3.1.2 Framing} Frames establish boundaries around payloads. Byte count is fragile if the count is damaged; sentinel bytes require byte stuffing; flag bit patterns require bit stuffing; physical coding violations can also mark boundaries.\item \textbf{3.1.3 Error control} Sequence numbers, acknowledgments, negative acknowledgments, and timers prevent silent loss and suppress duplicate delivery when a frame or acknowledgment is damaged.\item \textbf{3.1.4 Flow control} A fast sender must not overrun a slower receiver. Feedback or a window limits outstanding frames according to receiver capacity.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{3.2 Error detection and correction} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright Forward error correction spends redundancy to repair at the receiver; detection plus retransmission spends feedback and time.\par\begin{itemize}\item \textbf{3.2.1 Error-correcting codes} A codeword adds redundancy. With minimum Hamming distance $d$, up to $d-1$ bit errors are detectable and $\lfloor(d-1)/2\rfloor$ are correctable. Hamming, convolutional, Reed-Solomon, and low-density parity-check codes suit different error patterns and decoding costs.\item \textbf{3.2.2 Error-detecting codes} Parity offers limited detection; Internet-style checksums add words with one's-complement arithmetic; cyclic redundancy checks treat a bit string as a polynomial and transmit a remainder. CRCs efficiently detect common burst errors when the generator is chosen appropriately.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{3.3 Elementary protocols} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright Progressively relaxing assumptions reveals why acknowledgments, timers, and sequence numbers are necessary.\par\begin{itemize}\item \textbf{3.3.1 Utopian simplex protocol} With error-free transmission, an always-ready receiver, and unlimited processing, the sender may continuously pass frames downward. The deliberately unrealistic case isolates basic protocol structure.\item \textbf{3.3.2 Stop-and-wait on an error-free channel} The sender transmits one frame and waits for receiver permission before sending the next, providing flow control but leaving the link idle during the wait.\item \textbf{3.3.3 Stop-and-wait on a noisy channel} A timer triggers retransmission after loss. A one-bit sequence number lets the receiver distinguish a new frame from a duplicate caused by a lost acknowledgment.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{3.4 Sliding-window protocols} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright A sliding window combines flow control with pipelining. Larger windows improve utilization on long-delay links but require sequence space, timers, and buffers.\par\begin{itemize}\item \textbf{3.4.1 One-bit sliding window} Each side may send one numbered frame before acknowledgment. Piggybacking places an acknowledgment in a reverse-direction data frame, but a delayed acknowledgment still needs a timer policy.\item \textbf{3.4.2 Go-Back-N} Multiple frames may be outstanding. The receiver accepts only the next expected frame and uses cumulative acknowledgment; after a timeout, the sender retransmits the missing frame and all later frames in its window.\item \textbf{3.4.3 Selective repeat} The receiver buffers acceptable out-of-order frames and acknowledges them individually or selectively. The sender retransmits only missing frames. To avoid ambiguity after sequence-number wraparound, the window must not exceed half the sequence space.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{3.5 Example link protocols} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright Concrete links combine physical modulation with framing and control conventions visible to the network layer.\par\begin{itemize}\item \textbf{3.5.1 Packet over SONET} IP packets can be framed with PPP and mapped into SONET payloads. PPP supplies framing, link negotiation, and protocol identification over a synchronous optical transport.\item \textbf{3.5.2 ADSL} ADSL separates the line into many subchannels, adapts bit loading to channel quality, and carries data through link-specific convergence and framing mechanisms.\end{itemize}\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\cellcolor{CCue}\textbf{3.6 Summary} & \begin{minipage}[t]{\linewidth}\raggedright The data-link layer identifies frames and controls errors and rate across a single hop. Coding, acknowledgments, sequence numbers, timers, and windows are complementary tools selected according to delay, error pattern, and receiver capacity.\end{minipage} \\ \hline
\end{longtable}

\begin{legacybox}Packet over SONET and the presented ADSL architecture are retained as concrete examples of framing over carrier and subscriber facilities. Their inclusion does not imply that they are the only current link choices.\end{legacybox}

\notesection{Chapter synthesis}

\begin{overviewbox}\textbf{Cause-and-effect chain.} A raw bit stream has no message boundaries; framing creates frames; errors threaten correctness; codes detect or correct damage; acknowledgments and timers recover losses; sequence numbers suppress duplicates; windows regulate the sender and fill the path.\par
\textbf{Common confusions.} Error control protects correctness, while flow control protects receiver capacity. A checksum is not forward error correction. A cumulative acknowledgment does not prove that later out-of-order frames were stored.\par
\textbf{Derived insight.} Protocol state is the price of reliable pipelining: better utilization requires larger windows, more buffering, and careful handling of sequence wraparound.\end{overviewbox}

\notesection{Retrieval practice}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Why does byte or bit stuffing preserve framing transparency?
  \item Compare the three services a data-link layer may offer to the network layer.
  \item What detection and correction guarantees follow from minimum distance $d=5$?
  \item Why is a CRC well suited to detecting burst errors?
  \item What assumption makes the utopian simplex protocol unrealistic?
  \item How does a one-bit sequence number prevent duplicate delivery in stop-and-wait?
  \item Why is stop-and-wait inefficient on a high bandwidth-delay-product link?
  \item Trace Go-Back-N after one middle frame is lost but later frames arrive.
  \item Trace selective repeat under the same loss and identify its extra costs.
  \item Why must a selective-repeat window be bounded relative to its sequence space?
  \item Distinguish error control from flow control using a concrete failure in each case.
  \item What responsibilities do PPP and SONET have when IP is carried over an optical link?
\end{enumerate}
\begin{CornellSummaryBox}{Rapid-review Cornell summary}Frames provide boundaries and transparency. Redundant codes detect or correct corruption. ARQ combines acknowledgments, timers, retransmissions, and sequence numbers. Sliding windows regulate flow and pipeline data; Go-Back-N favors simple receivers, while selective repeat trades buffers and state for fewer retransmissions.\end{CornellSummaryBox}
