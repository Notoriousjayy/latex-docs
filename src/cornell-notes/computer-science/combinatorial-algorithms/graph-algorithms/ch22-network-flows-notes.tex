\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=Teal!5,colframe=Teal,title=Essential question]How do layered residual networks and preflow pushout/pushback phases construct a maximum flow together with a certifying minimum cut?\end{tcolorbox}

\section*{Cornell notes}
\begin{longtable}{@{}Q!{\color{Mist}\vrule width 1pt}A@{}}\cornellhead
\cue{What is a network?}{A directed-capacity graph has source $s$, sink $t$, and capacities $c_{pq}\ge0$. The skew-symmetric representation uses $\phi_{pq}=-\phi_{qp}$. A feasible flow obeys capacity limits and has zero net imbalance at every vertex other than source and sink.}
\cue{What is a cut certificate?}{A cut $S$ contains $s$ and excludes $t$, with capacity $\sum_{p\in S,q\notin S}c_{pq}$. Flow conservation shows the value of any feasible flow equals its net flow across any cut and cannot exceed cut capacity. Equality between one flow and one cut proves both are optimal.}
\cue{What is residual capacity?}{After current flow $\phi$, residual capacity is $\bar c_{pq}=c_{pq}-\phi_{pq}$. It records how much more can move from $p$ to $q$, including the ability to cancel prior flow through the reverse direction.}
\cue{What is a KZ-net?}{At the start of a phase, form an acyclic directed subgraph of positive-residual edges containing all shortest residual source-to-sink paths. The source is its unique minimum and sink its unique maximum in the induced partial order. When a phase saturates or blocks all its paths, the next phase has strictly longer shortest paths, so at most $n$ phases occur.}
\cue{What is a preflow?}{A preflow obeys capacity bounds but allows intermediate vertices to retain nonnegative excess. A forward pushout sends as much incoming excess as possible along outgoing layered edges, filling one edge before using the next.}
\cue{Why are pushbacks needed?}{Some vertices cannot forward all excess. Retreat processes such vertices in reverse compatible order, moves unsent excess backward, and closes unusable edges. Old flow is safeguarded separately from newly introduced preflow. When all intermediate excesses vanish, the preflow is a feasible flow for the phase.}
\cue{How does the phase prove progress?}{After advance and retreat, every source-to-sink path in the KZ-net contains an edge saturated by the phase flow. Therefore no shortest residual path of the same length remains. Rebuilding residual capacities either finds a longer layered net or proves the sink unreachable.}
\cue{What does NETFLO return?}{The flow is stored in the second capacity/flow row. A cut indicator marks vertices reachable from the source in the final residual graph. The equality of final flow value and cut capacity is the optimality certificate.}
\cue{Applications}{\begin{itemize}\item Bipartite maximum matching and minimum vertex cover.\item Systems of distinct representatives.\item Dilworth chain covering and antichains.\item Edge connectivity through repeated unit-capacity flows.\item Existence and construction of $0$-$1$ matrices with prescribed row and column sums.\end{itemize}}
\cue{Cost and cautions}{The source bounds KZ construction and aggregate pushbacks in one phase by $O(E)$, with additional vertex/edge control; across at most $n$ phases it states a cubic-order bound in the dense case. Input lists both directions of every edge, capacities are integral in the implementation, and endpoint/capacity arrays are destructively sorted and cross-referenced.}
\cue{Retrieval practice}{\begin{enumerate}\item What equality certifies optimality?\item What does residual capacity mean?\item Why is the KZ-net acyclic?\item How does a preflow differ from a flow?\item What ends a phase?\item How is a matching encoded?\end{enumerate}}
\end{longtable}
\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=Ink!4,colframe=Ink,title=Cornell summary]
NETFLO constructs an optimal flow in phases. Each phase builds an acyclic KZ-net containing the shortest positive-residual source-to-sink paths. Forward pushouts distribute preflow and may leave excess at blocked vertices; reverse retreats push that excess back until conservation is restored. The resulting phase flow blocks every path in the layered net, so the next residual phase must use longer paths. When the sink is unreachable, source-reachable vertices define a cut whose capacity equals the flow value, certifying both maximum flow and minimum cut. The routine's cross-referenced, destructively sorted edge representation supports applications ranging from bipartite matching to prescribed-margin $0$-$1$ matrices, but demands strict input symmetry and careful capacity/flow bookkeeping.
\end{tcolorbox}
