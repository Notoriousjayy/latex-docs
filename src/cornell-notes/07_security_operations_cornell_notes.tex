\section*{\color{navy}Domain Overview}
Security operations keeps controls effective under real-world conditions. It combines defensible investigations, monitoring, configuration, privilege/resource protection, incident handling, preventive/detective operations, patch and vulnerability workflows, controlled change, recovery and disaster processes, facility safeguards, and human safety.

\begin{summarybox}{Review Objectives}
Preserve digital evidence; operate logging/SIEM and threat monitoring; maintain secure baselines; manage privilege/media/incidents; operate detective/preventive controls; run patch/vulnerability/change processes; design backups and recovery sites against RTO/RPO/MTD; test DR plans; and protect facilities and personnel.
\end{summarybox}

\section{\color{navy}Investigations and Digital Forensics}
\source{pp. 464--477}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Evidence principle}{Evidence supports a factual proposition; one artifact rarely proves the entire conclusion. Collect corroborating sources and preserve context/metadata so investigators can explain what the evidence establishes and what it does not.}
\noterow{Digital evidence collection}{Document dates/times/locations/actions and personnel; preserve volatile/transient data when needed; create bit-level forensic copies where appropriate; analyze copies instead of originals; use write blockers; and hash evidence to verify integrity.}
\noterow{Why not power off immediately?}{Volatile RAM, processes, connections, caches, and encryption material may disappear on shutdown. Balance containment with preservation; network isolation can sometimes stop harm while retaining evidence that power loss would destroy.}
\noterow{Chain of custody}{Document possession, transfer, storage, access, and controlled changes from collection through disposition. Chain of custody supports integrity/authenticity and accountability; unexplained gaps can undermine reliance on evidence.}
\noterow{Reporting and documentation}{Record contemporaneous facts, commands/tools, observations, timestamps, locations, personnel, hashes, and evidence identifiers. Tailor final reporting to incident, management, regulator, or court needs without changing the underlying factual record.}
\noterow{Evidence quality}{Evidence should be relevant to the issue, authentic/reliably sourced, sufficiently complete, accurate, and handled according to applicable legal/organizational rules. Court use requires especially rigorous procedures and qualified forensic practice.}
\noterow{Investigative techniques}{Use examination, interviews, log/data correlation, timelines, searches, forensic acquisition/analysis, and controlled testing as appropriate. Preserve objectivity and separate observed fact from inference.}
\noterow{Forensics tools/tactics}{Forensic work may examine disks/filesystems, memory, network traffic, mobile devices, cloud records, applications, and logs. Tools must be validated/understood, analysis reproducible, and originals protected from modification.}
\noterow{Artifacts and Locard's principle}{Interactions tend to leave traces. File metadata, logs, registry/configuration data, browser/history, memory/processes, network records, authentication events, and device/cloud telemetry can corroborate activity. Collect artifacts before normal retention/rotation removes them.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Logging and Monitoring}
\source{pp. 478--488}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{IDS vs. IPS}{An \textbf{IDS} detects and alerts; an \textbf{IPS} is positioned/configured to take preventive action such as blocking. Network- and host-based approaches use signatures, behavior/anomaly, or combinations. Tune both false positives and false negatives.}
\noterow{SIEM}{Security Information and Event Management centralizes log/event collection, normalization, correlation, search, alerting, dashboards, and retention. Its value depends on quality data, synchronized time, meaningful detection logic, trained responders, and continuous tuning.}
\noterow{Continuous monitoring}{Replace exclusive reliance on periodic snapshots with recurring/automated observation of control state and risk. Monitor assets, vulnerabilities, configuration, identities, threats, security events, and control performance at a cadence matching change and impact.}
\noterow{Egress monitoring}{Watch outbound traffic for data theft, command-and-control, malware callbacks, policy violations, and unusual destinations/volume. DLP, proxies, DNS/network analytics, firewalls, and endpoint telemetry can detect exfiltration paths.}
\noterow{Log management lifecycle}{Define sources/events, collect centrally, normalize/time-sync, protect integrity/confidentiality, control access, analyze/alert, retain per requirement, archive, and dispose. Avoid recording secrets/PII unnecessarily; logs themselves can become sensitive evidence.}
\noterow{Threat intelligence}{Collect and analyze information about actors, tactics/techniques, vulnerabilities, infrastructure, indicators, and campaigns. Evaluate source reliability/relevance/timeliness, enrich internal context, and turn intelligence into preventive/detective/prioritization actions.}
\noterow{UEBA}{User and Entity Behavior Analytics establishes behavioral baselines and detects anomalies among users, service accounts, devices, or other entities. It helps identify compromised accounts/insiders but requires context, tuning, privacy governance, and human validation.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Configuration and Foundational Operations}
\source{pp. 489--499}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Configuration management (CM)}{Define, approve, document, implement, verify, and track system configuration over the lifecycle. CM establishes known state and makes deviations visible, enabling repeatability, auditability, safe change, and faster recovery.}
\noterow{Provisioning}{Deploy systems/resources using approved hardware/software and baseline configuration. Images, templates, infrastructure automation, and cloud provisioning improve consistency; protect the templates/pipelines because defects can replicate at scale.}
\noterow{Asset inventory}{Maintain hardware, software, service, cloud, ownership, location, version, and lifecycle records. Reconcile automated discovery with authoritative inventories; unmanaged/unknown assets cannot reliably be patched, monitored, or retired.}
\noterow{Baselining}{An agreed set of system attributes/configuration becomes the reference state. Harden baselines, version them, approve deviations, measure drift, and update deliberately as threats, software, and requirements change.}
\noterow{Automation}{Automation improves speed and consistency for provisioning, configuration, monitoring, patching, response, and compliance. It also amplifies errors and privilege; secure code/repos/secrets, use testing/approvals, least-privilege service identities, logging, and rollback.}
\noterow{Need-to-know / least privilege}{Need-to-know limits access to information necessary for a task; least privilege limits permissions/capabilities to the minimum required. Review both as roles and systems change.}
\noterow{Separation of duties}{Split incompatible/high-risk activities so one person cannot authorize and execute the entire transaction. Combine with dual control for especially sensitive functions and monitor for collusion or role accumulation.}
\noterow{Privileged Account Management}{Apply stronger request/approval, separate admin identities, MFA, vaulting/rotation, JIT/time limits, session control/recording, command/activity logging, reviews, and prompt deprovisioning. Standing privilege increases blast radius.}
\noterow{Job rotation}{Cross-trains staff, improves continuity, and can expose long-running fraud/errors by placing another person in the role. Rotation complements, rather than replaces, separation of duties and mandatory vacations.}
\noterow{Service-level agreements}{Define measurable service expectations, roles, availability/performance, incident/support response, recovery, security obligations, reporting, escalation, and remedies. Monitor actual service against the SLA.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Resource Protection and Incident Management}
\source{pp. 499--511}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Media management}{Inventory and label media; control issuance/use/transport/storage; match handling to classification; encrypt where appropriate; track custody; protect backups; and sanitize/destroy at end of life. Removable media introduces easy exfiltration and malware paths.}
\noterow{Media protection techniques}{Use locked/environmentally controlled storage, access logs, encryption/key management, write protection, secure transport containers/couriers, redundant/offsite copies, retention rules, and media-appropriate sanitization. Periodically test readability and migrate obsolete media.}
\noterow{Incident management plan}{Predefine scope, incident categories/severity, roles/authority, contacts, escalation, communications, evidence procedures, containment/recovery playbooks, external coordination, tools, training/exercises, and post-incident improvement. Plans must exist before the crisis.}
\noterow{Detection}{Identify potential incidents through user reports, monitoring, SIEM/IDS/EDR alerts, logs, threat intelligence, anomalies, and third-party notice. Validate evidence and classify severity rather than treating every alert as an incident.}
\noterow{Response / triage}{Confirm, prioritize, assign ownership, preserve evidence, communicate, and choose initial containment based on impact. Triage focuses scarce resources on the most consequential incidents and prevents premature destructive action.}
\noterow{Mitigation / containment}{Stop spread and reduce immediate harm using isolation, credential disable/reset, filtering, temporary configuration, process termination, or segmentation. Short-term containment is not necessarily the permanent remediation.}
\noterow{Reporting}{Notify internal leadership, legal/privacy/compliance, customers/partners, insurers, regulators, or law enforcement as required. Use authorized communications channels and meet statutory/contractual timeframes without speculation.}
\noterow{Recovery}{Restore trustworthy operation from repaired/rebuilt systems and validated data, monitor for recurrence, and remove temporary containment only when safe. Recovery can begin during response but must meet business priorities and security validation.}
\noterow{Remediation}{Eliminate root cause and durable weaknesses: patch, redesign, reconfigure, rotate credentials, improve process/training, or replace vulnerable components. Verify effectiveness through testing/monitoring.}
\noterow{Lessons learned}{After stabilization, document timeline, decisions, what worked/failed, root causes, control gaps, communication issues, metrics, costs, and action owners/dates. Feed improvements into controls, architecture, plans, detection, training, and risk records.}
\end{cornell}

\begin{exambox}{Incident Sequence Cue}
Detection and triage lead to response/containment; recovery restores service; remediation removes root causes; lessons learned improves the system. In practice activities overlap, but do not mistake temporary containment for complete remediation.
\end{exambox}

\section{\color{navy}Detective and Preventive Operations}
\source{pp. 511--518}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Firewalls}{Enforce network-boundary traffic policy. Maintain least-privilege rule sets, change approvals, periodic rule review, secure administration, logging, patching, capacity, and appropriate fail behavior. Internal segmentation is as important as the perimeter.}
\noterow{IDS / IPS operations}{Maintain signatures/models, sensor coverage, time sync, alert routing, baseline/tuning, response playbooks, and update lifecycle. IPS actions can disrupt legitimate traffic, so prevention rules require confidence and rollback/exception handling.}
\noterow{Allowlisting / denylisting}{Allowlisting permits only explicitly approved items and is stronger when a complete trusted set is practical. Denylisting blocks known bad items but cannot enumerate unknown threats. Combine with reputation, behavior, and least privilege.}
\noterow{Third-party security services}{Managed detection, SOC, anti-DDoS, cloud security, and other services can add expertise/scale. Contracts must address access, data, logging, response, notification, evidence, subcontractors, SLA, compliance, and exit/transition.}
\noterow{Sandboxing}{Execute untrusted/suspicious code/content in an isolated environment to restrict effects and observe behavior. Sandboxes need strong isolation and can be detected/escaped; treat results as evidence, not absolute proof of safety.}
\noterow{Honeypots / honeynets}{Decoy systems/networks attract or reveal malicious activity and can collect intelligence. Isolate them so they cannot become a pivot, govern monitoring/evidence, and avoid legal/ethical designs that encourage harm beyond observation.}
\noterow{Anti-malware / EDR}{Signature detection identifies known patterns; heuristics/behavior detect suspicious activity; endpoint detection/response adds telemetry and response. Keep engines/rules current, manage exclusions, investigate alerts, and layer with hardening/least privilege.}
\noterow{ML / AI tools}{Models can find complex/anomalous patterns at scale but inherit training-data quality, bias, explainability, evasion, false-positive, and model-drift issues. Measure detection value and retain human oversight for consequential decisions.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Patch, Vulnerability, and Change Management}
\source{pp. 519--522}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Patch management}{Inventory supported software, obtain trusted patches, assess applicability/risk, test, prioritize, schedule/approve, deploy, verify installation/function, monitor issues, and maintain rollback/emergency processes. Prioritize exploited/high-impact exposure rather than patch-count alone.}
\noterow{Vulnerability management}{Continuously discover assets, identify vulnerabilities, validate/contextualize, prioritize by exploitability/asset/risk, assign remediation, track SLA/age, verify fixes through rescanning/testing, manage exceptions, and report trends/root causes.}
\noterow{Patch vs. vulnerability management}{Patching is one remediation method. Vulnerability management is the broader risk workflow and may require configuration changes, compensating controls, removal, upgrades, code fixes, segmentation, or formal acceptance when no patch exists.}
\noterow{Change management}{Require documented request, business/security impact and risk, testing, approvals/separation, implementation plan/window, communications, rollback, validation, and updated configuration/documentation. Emergency changes need expedited control plus retrospective review.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Recovery Strategies}
\source{pp. 523--528}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{RTO}{\textbf{Recovery Time Objective}: target elapsed time after disruption before a process/system reaches the defined contingency/minimum acceptable service. It should be shorter than maximum tolerable downtime.}
\noterow{RPO}{\textbf{Recovery Point Objective}: maximum tolerable data loss, expressed as time, transactions, or data points. Backup/replication frequency and architecture must be capable of meeting it.}
\noterow{MTD / MAD}{\textbf{Maximum Tolerable/Allowable Downtime}: longest the organization can survive without the process/asset. If RTO exceeds MTD, planned recovery arrives too late to preserve viability.}
\noterow{Full backup}{Copies all selected data; easiest restore baseline but highest backup time/storage for each run. Use when schedule/cost can support it and combine with other types to meet RPO/RTO.}
\noterow{Differential backup}{Copies all data changed since the last full backup. Restore typically needs the last full + latest differential; differentials grow until the next full.}
\noterow{Incremental backup}{Copies changes since the most recent full or incremental. Small/fast backup windows, but restoration usually needs the full plus every subsequent incremental in order.}
\noterow{Snapshots / versions / archives}{Can support recovery when independently protected and consistent, but a snapshot on the same compromised/failing infrastructure may not meet backup/resilience needs. Match retention, immutability/offsite separation, and restore testing to threats.}
\noterow{RAID cue}{RAID 0 = striping/performance, no fault tolerance; RAID 1 = mirroring; RAID 5 = striping + parity; RAID 0+1 and 1+0 combine striping/mirroring differently. RAID improves availability/performance but is not a substitute for backups.}
\noterow{Cold / warm / hot site}{\textbf{Cold}: facility with little/no operational equipment, cheapest/slowest recovery. \textbf{Warm}: partially equipped/prepared. \textbf{Hot}: infrastructure and current data closely mirror production, fastest/highest cost. Choose from business RTO/RPO and correlated-disaster risk.}
\noterow{Multiple processing sites}{Geographic diversity, active/active or active/passive designs, cloud regions/zones, and provider strategies can improve availability. Ensure dependencies, identity/data replication, networks, and staff are not exposed to the same single event.}
\noterow{Resilience / HA / QoS / fault tolerance}{\textbf{Resilience} anticipates and recovers from failure. \textbf{HA} uses redundancy/failover to minimize outage. \textbf{QoS} prioritizes important/time-sensitive traffic. \textbf{Fault tolerance} continues operation despite defined component failure. Requirements and cost follow business criticality.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Disaster Recovery and Business Continuity}
\source{pp. 529--538}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{DR vs. BC}{Disaster Recovery focuses on restoring IT/information/telecommunications after disruption. Business Continuity focuses on sustaining the organization's business operations. DR supports BC; both are driven by BIA priorities and recovery objectives.}
\noterow{DR response and declaration}{Authorized leadership determines when conditions meet disaster criteria and activates the plan. Response shifts from normal/incident processes to approved contingency procedures and alternate capabilities.}
\noterow{Personnel}{Predefine teams, authority, alternates, contact methods, safety/accountability, workspace/remote arrangements, and succession. Plans that depend on one unavailable expert are fragile.}
\noterow{Communications}{Maintain redundant contact methods and current stakeholder/vendor/emergency information. Assign authorized spokespersons; internal, customer, regulator, partner, and media communications require consistent verified information.}
\noterow{Assessment}{Determine damage, affected services/dependencies, safety conditions, recovery feasibility, data integrity, and priority. Reassess as facts change; do not restore into an unsafe or still-compromised environment.}
\noterow{Restoration}{Recover prioritized services/data using backups/alternate sites, validate security/integrity, reconcile transactions, transition users/dependencies, monitor, and eventually return to stable normal operation in a controlled manner.}
\noterow{Training / awareness}{Personnel must know responsibilities, communications, locations, manual workarounds, and escalation. Train before the disaster and update after organizational/system change.}
\noterow{DR lessons learned}{After exercises/events, update plans, inventories, contacts, recovery procedures, architecture, contracts, training, and risk records. Assign and track corrective actions rather than only issuing an after-action report.}
\noterow{Read-through / tabletop}{Least disruptive. Participants review the plan or role-play a scenario verbally to find stale contacts, missing steps, assumptions, and coordination gaps. Cheap enough to run frequently.}
\noterow{Walkthrough}{Participants physically follow key response steps/locations without fully switching production, validating travel, facilities, equipment, and procedural assumptions.}
\noterow{Simulation}{Exercise response to a realistic scenario (for example fire drill or alternate-facility/data restoration) without necessarily interrupting live operations. Tests coordination and selected technical capabilities.}
\noterow{Parallel test}{Operate alternate systems/site alongside production under realistic load. Provides strong evidence with lower production risk than full interruption, but costs resources and can reveal replication/configuration gaps.}
\noterow{Full interruption}{Switch production to contingency/alternate procedures as if disaster is real. Provides the strongest end-to-end test but has highest business risk/cost; scope carefully, secure leadership approval, and have rollback/safety measures.}
\noterow{BC planning/exercises}{Exercise the business workarounds, staffing, suppliers, facilities, communications, and customer/regulatory functions that continue beyond IT restoration. Ensure dependencies between business processes and DR capabilities are explicit.}
\end{cornell}

\begin{exambox}{Recovery Logic}
Business impact sets RPO, RTO, and MTD. Technology is then selected to meet them. A hot site or near-zero RPO is not automatically better if the business does not justify the cost.
\end{exambox}

\section{\color{navy}Physical Security and Personnel Safety}
\source{pp. 539--548}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Perimeter security}{Use site boundaries, fences/barriers/bollards, lighting, CCTV/sensors, guards, controlled vehicle/pedestrian entrances, signage, and visitor processes according to risk. Layer deterrence, detection, delay, and response.}
\noterow{Internal security}{Use zones, locks/badges/biometrics, mantraps where appropriate, restricted rooms/racks, visitor escort, alarms/surveillance, clean desk/screen, and separation of public/work/sensitive areas. Review physical access as roles change.}
\noterow{Travel security}{Prepare travelers for device/data minimization, secure communications/VPN, local laws/customs, border/search risk, hotel/public-network threats, physical theft, emergency contacts, and reporting. Higher-risk destinations may require dedicated loaner devices.}
\noterow{Security training and awareness}{Teach physical and personal safety: badge/visitor challenge, tailgating, phishing/social engineering, emergency response, suspicious activity, device/media handling, and reporting without encouraging unsafe confrontation.}
\noterow{Emergency management}{Life safety precedes asset protection. Coordinate evacuation/shelter, accountability, first aid, emergency services, communications, accessible procedures, drills, and continuity; define who has authority to act.}
\noterow{Duress}{A person may be forced to act against their will. Use discreet code words, duress PINs/alarms, escalation, and training that detects coercion without increasing danger to the victim.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}High-Yield Review}
\begin{itemize}
\item Preserve evidence through documentation, copies, write blocking, hashing, and chain of custody.
\item Centralized monitoring is useful only when logs are complete, protected, time-synchronized, and converted into response.
\item Baselines and automation create repeatability; protect the automation because errors scale.
\item Privilege and removable media need explicit ownership, lifecycle, review, and logging.
\item Incident containment is temporary; remediation removes root cause; lessons learned closes the improvement loop.
\item RTO \(<\) MTD; RPO drives tolerated data loss; recovery architecture follows business objectives.
\item DR test realism/risk increases roughly from tabletop to walkthrough to simulation to parallel to full interruption.
\item Life safety comes before systems and evidence when priorities conflict.
\end{itemize}

\subsection*{\color{teal}Answer Key}
\begin{enumerate}
\item Document everything, collect volatile data appropriately, make forensic/bit copies, use write blockers, hash/verify, control transfers/storage/access, and analyze copies rather than originals.
\item IDS detects; IPS can block; SIEM centralizes/correlates events; continuous monitoring repeatedly measures control/risk state; UEBA detects behavioral anomalies.
\item It defines approved known state, enables consistent provisioning, detects drift, supports audit, and accelerates recovery.
\item Containment stops spread; recovery restores trustworthy service; remediation eliminates root cause/durable weakness.
\item Vulnerability management includes discovery, context/prioritization, multiple treatment options, exceptions, ownership, SLA, and verification; patching is one treatment.
\item RTO = recovery-time target; RPO = tolerable data loss; MTD = maximum survivable outage; RTO should be less than MTD.
\item Full alone contains all selected data; differential restore needs full + latest differential; incremental needs full + all incrementals since it.
\item Cold = least ready/slow/cheap; warm = partial; hot = highly ready/current/fast/expensive.
\item RAID can improve availability but does not protect against many logical corruption, deletion, ransomware, site-loss, or retention scenarios.
\item Read-through/tabletop, walkthrough, simulation, parallel, full interruption.
\item DR restores IT/information/communications; BC sustains business operations and includes DR as a dependency.
\item Human life/safety.
\end{enumerate}

\section{\color{navy}Cornell Summary}
\begin{summarybox}{Domain 7 Summary}
Security operations maintains trustworthy state while systems, threats, people, and incidents change. Preserve evidence defensibly, monitor continuously, keep inventories and baselines current, constrain privilege and media, respond to incidents without confusing containment with remediation, and verify patches/changes. When disruption exceeds normal response, recovery objectives derived from business impact determine backups, alternate sites, resilience, and DR procedures. Exercise those plans progressively and remember that human safety outranks protection of technology or evidence.
\end{summarybox}

\section{\color{navy}Final Domain Checklist}
\begin{itemize}[label=\(\square\)]
\item I can preserve, analyze, and document digital evidence.
\item I can operate IDS/IPS, SIEM, continuous/egress monitoring, logs, threat intel, and UEBA.
\item I can manage provisioning, inventory, baselines, automation, privilege, SLAs, and media.
\item I can run the complete incident-management lifecycle and operational controls.
\item I can execute patch, vulnerability, and change management.
\item I can design backups/sites/resilience to RTO, RPO, and MTD.
\item I can run DR/BC processes and distinguish all five DR test types.
\item I can implement facility controls while prioritizing personnel safety, travel, emergencies, and duress.
\end{itemize}
