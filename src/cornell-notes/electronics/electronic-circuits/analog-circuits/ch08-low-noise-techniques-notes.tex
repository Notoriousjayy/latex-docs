\begin{CornellOverviewBox}{Concept}
Low-noise design treats random device noise and deterministic interference as different problems. Noise must be expressed as a spectral density, integrated over the actual transfer function, and referred to a common point. Source impedance then guides the choice among BJT, JFET, op-amp, transformer, and transimpedance front ends, while shielding, grounding, bandwidth control, and measurement technique determine whether theoretical performance is visible.
\end{CornellOverviewBox}
\begin{CornellOverviewBox}{Concept}
\textbf{Prerequisites.} RMS quantities, decibels, filter transfer functions, transistor/op-amp small-signal models, and basic probability concepts.

\textbf{After studying this chapter, you should be able to:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Calculate thermal, shot, voltage, and current noise over bandwidth.
  \item Choose an input device by matching voltage/current noise to source impedance and frequency.
  \item Design stable low-noise voltage and transimpedance amplifiers.
  \item Separate intrinsic noise from interference and measure both with calibrated bandwidth.
\end{itemize}\textbf{Central design questions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item What is the source impedance versus frequency and which noise mechanism dominates there?
  \item What exact transfer function and equivalent noise bandwidth integrate the spectrum?
  \item Is an observed line random noise, coupled interference, oscillation, or measurement artifact?
\end{itemize}\end{CornellOverviewBox}
\section{Cornell Notes}
\begin{CornellNotesTable}
\CornellNoteRow{8.1: Noise mechanisms}{Johnson noise arises from resistance: $e_n=\sqrt{4kTR}$ V/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. Shot noise arises from discrete charge flow: $i_n=\sqrt{2qI}$ A/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. Flicker noise rises at low frequency; burst noise is non-Gaussian and device dependent. Random sources combine by power/RSS when uncorrelated. Interference is coupled energy and should be traced, shielded, balanced, filtered, or eliminated rather than folded into a white-noise number.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.2: SNR and noise figure}{Noise density becomes rms only after integration through $|H(f)|^2$. SNR compares defined signal power or rms to noise in the same bandwidth. Noise factor $F=SNR_{in}/SNR_{out}$ and $NF=10\log_{10}F$ describe degradation relative to a source at a specified temperature; they can hide the actual voltage/current-noise trade for unusual source impedances.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.3--8.5: BJT noise}{BJT base spreading resistance contributes voltage noise; base and collector currents contribute shot noise. Increasing collector current can reduce input voltage noise but raises current noise and power. For low source resistance a BJT often excels. Use $e_n$ and $i_nR_S$ to compare contributions, include source thermal noise, bias network noise, and frequency dependence, then select current near the system optimum rather than a universal value.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.4: From noise figure to $e_n$}{Noise-figure curves depend on source resistance, current, frequency, and reference temperature. Convert carefully by reconstructing source thermal noise and amplifier-added noise; do not read one NF number as a broadband input-voltage density. Confirm whether matching networks, device gain, and test fixture are included.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.6--8.7: JFET comparison}{JFETs offer very low current noise and high input resistance, making them favorable for high-impedance or capacitive sources. Voltage noise improves with transconductance, often through larger device area or parallel devices, but input capacitance rises. MOSFET flicker noise can be substantial. Plot total amplifier noise versus source impedance and frequency to find crossovers rather than declaring one device family universally quieter.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.8--8.9: Differential and op-amp noise}{Include each input's voltage/current noise, resistor thermal noise, noise gain, source impedance, and correlations when specified. In an inverting op-amp circuit, input-current noise flows through its impedance while voltage noise is amplified by noise gain. PSRR converts supply noise, and CMRR converts common-mode interference. Tables of `low noise' parts are meaningful only with source and band.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.10: Signal transformers}{A transformer can transform source impedance toward an amplifier's optimum without adding active-device noise, but winding resistance adds thermal noise and core/capacitance limit bandwidth and linearity. Turns ratio scales voltage and impedance squared. Check magnetizing inductance at low frequency, leakage/capacitance at high frequency, saturation, shielding, and pickup.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.11: Transimpedance amplifiers}{A TIA converts sensor current with $V_o=-I_sR_f$. Input capacitance and op-amp voltage noise create rising noise gain and possible instability. Choose feedback capacitance for phase margin and bandwidth, include detector/board/amplifier capacitance, and integrate $e_nC$ noise. Cascoding or bootstrapping may reduce effective input capacitance but adds complexity and noise paths.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.12: Noise measurement}{Measure with known gain, calibrated analyzer bandwidth/window, low-noise source termination, shielding, and enough averaging. Shorted-input results may omit current-noise interaction with source impedance. A known noise source or resistor can calibrate gain and bandwidth. Avoid analyzer floor, overload, aliasing, ground loops, and spectral leakage. Record whether values are density, rms, peak, or peak-to-peak.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.13: Bandwidth and RMS}{Integrated noise is $v_{rms}=\sqrt{\int e_n^2(f)|H(f)|^2df}$. For flat density through an ideal brick-wall band $B$, this becomes $e_n\sqrt{B}$; real filters use equivalent noise bandwidth. To locate a $1/f$ corner, compare low-frequency rise to the white floor with a known filter response. RMS meters need adequate crest factor and bandwidth.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.14: Narrowband improvement}{Narrowing bandwidth improves SNR for a coherent narrowband signal against broadband noise. A lock-in multiplies by a reference and low-pass filters, translating the desired phase component to dc while rejecting other frequencies. Equivalent noise bandwidth and integration time are linked; longer averaging does not remove coherent interference at the reference or drift.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.15: Supply noise}{Supply ripple couples through finite PSRR, bias networks, ground impedance, and electric/magnetic fields. Use quiet references, local decoupling, RC/LC filtering, low-noise regulation, and separated return paths. A capacitance multiplier reduces ripple but consumes headroom and does not provide ideal regulation. Measure supply-to-output transfer across frequency.}
\CornellNoteRow{8.16: Shielding and grounding}{Electric fields are reduced by conductive shields tied deliberately; magnetic pickup is reduced by loop-area control, twisting, distance, and high-permeability shielding where appropriate. Differential signaling rejects shared interference only within CMRR and balance limits. Ground is an impedance network. Separate noisy current paths, use star or plane strategies appropriate to frequency, and avoid multiple unintended instrument-earth paths.}
\end{CornellNotesTable}
\begin{CornellSummaryBox}{Summary}
Noise performance follows from source impedance, device voltage/current noise, resistor and bias noise, transfer functions, and bandwidth. Interference, grounding, and measurement artifacts require physical mitigation. The lowest data-sheet voltage-noise part is not necessarily the quietest system; the correct design minimizes total input-referred noise while remaining stable, linear, and measurable.
\end{CornellSummaryBox}
\section{Worked Examples}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Resistor noise}
At $T=300\,\mathrm{K}$, a $10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ resistor has $e_n=\sqrt{4kTR}=12.9\,\mathrm{nV}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. Through an ideal $5\,\mathrm{kHz}$ band, rms is $12.9\sqrt{5000}=0.912\,\mu\mathrm{V}$. A real first-order filter with the same corner has a different equivalent noise bandwidth.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Voltage/current noise crossover}
An amplifier has $e_n=4\,\mathrm{nV}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ and $i_n=0.8\,\mathrm{pA}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. Their contributions are equal when $R_S=e_n/i_n=5\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$. Below this, voltage noise tends to dominate; above it, current noise tends to dominate, before source thermal noise and capacitance are included.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Photodiode shot noise}
A photodiode dc current is $40\,\mu\mathrm{A}$. Shot density is $\sqrt{2qI}=3.58\,\mathrm{pA}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. With $R_f=220\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ and ideal $2\,\mathrm{kHz}$ bandwidth, its output rms contribution is $3.58\,\mathrm{pA}\sqrt{2000}(220\,\mathrm{k\Omega})=35.2\,\mu\mathrm{V}$, excluding resistor and amplifier noise.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\section{Design and Troubleshooting}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Design and Troubleshooting}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define signal, source impedance/capacitance, frequency band, required SNR, dynamic range, and interference environment.
  \item Refer every random source to input or output, include transfer functions, and integrate with equivalent noise bandwidth.
  \item Select device/topology, bias, impedances, feedback compensation, filtering, shielding, grounding, and supply strategy together.
  \item Measure noise density and integrated rms with calibrated bandwidth; vary termination, shielding, power, and temperature to identify mechanisms.
\end{enumerate}\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Symptoms, likely causes, and checks}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Flat floor too high: resistor noise, wrong source impedance, amplifier $e_n/i_n$, analyzer floor, or excess bandwidth.
  \item Low-frequency rise: flicker, drift, microphonics, thermal gradients, leakage, or insufficient measurement duration.
  \item Narrow spectral lines: supply/clock pickup, ground loop, radio coupling, vibration, or oscillation -- not white noise.
  \item TIA peaking/oscillation: underestimated input capacitance, too little feedback capacitance, poor layout, or unsuitable op amp.
  \item Noise changes with cable position: magnetic/electric pickup, triboelectric cable noise, or grounding path changes.
\end{itemize}\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellWarningBox}{Safety and Limitations}
Low-noise experiments often use floating shields, batteries, or isolated instruments. Do not defeat protective earth or create exposed hazardous nodes to reduce hum. Use approved differential/isolation methods. Photodiode and detector bias supplies can be high voltage; current-limit, discharge, shield, and label them.
\end{CornellWarningBox}
\section{Key-Equation Sheet}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Key Equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Thermal voltage density: $e_n=\sqrt{4kTR}$ V/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$.
  \item Shot current density: $i_n=\sqrt{2qI}$ A/$\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$.
  \item Uncorrelated combination: $e_{tot}=\sqrt{\sum e_i^2}$ after referring terms to one point.
  \item Integrated noise: $v_{rms}=\sqrt{\int_0^{\infty}e_n^2(f)|H(f)|^2df}$.
  \item Noise factor: $F=SNR_{in}/SNR_{out}$; $NF=10\log_{10}F$.
  \item TIA signal gain: $V_o=-I_sR_f$ at frequencies where loop gain maintains the virtual node.
\end{itemize}\end{CornellExamBox}
\section{Study and Review}
\subsection{Design checklist}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Source impedance/capacitance and measurement band are explicit.
  \item Random noise and coherent interference are separated.
  \item All densities are integrated through actual transfer functions.
  \item TIA/noise-gain stability and dynamic range are verified.
  \item Analyzer floor, calibration, grounding, shielding, and reporting units are documented.
\end{itemize}\subsection{Common misconceptions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Noise density is not an rms voltage until bandwidth is applied.
  \item Shorting an input does not test current-noise interaction with a real source.
  \item Lowest $e_n$ does not guarantee lowest total system noise.
  \item A ground symbol does not remove ground impedance or loops.
  \item Averaging does not eliminate coherent interference or drift.
\end{itemize}\subsection{Active-recall questions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write thermal and shot-noise density equations.
  \item When may noise powers be added?
  \item What is equivalent noise bandwidth?
  \item How does source impedance guide BJT versus JFET choice?
  \item What is the $e_nC$ problem in a TIA?
  \item Why can a transformer improve noise match?
  \item How are noise factor and noise figure related?
  \item What distinguishes $1/f$ noise from line interference?
  \item How does a lock-in improve SNR?
  \item Which test identifies a ground loop?
\end{enumerate}\subsection{Original practice problems}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Find thermal density of $1\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ at $300\,\mathrm{K}$.
  \item Two uncorrelated input noises are $5$ and $12\,\mathrm{nV}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$. Combine them.
  \item A flat $20\,\mathrm{nV}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ density passes through $1\,\mathrm{kHz}$ ideal bandwidth with gain 50. Find output rms.
\end{enumerate}\subsection{Answer key / solution outline}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $4.07\,\mathrm{nV}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$.
  \item $\sqrt{5^2+12^2}=13\,\mathrm{nV}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$.
  \item Input rms is $0.632\,\mu\mathrm{V}$; output rms is $31.6\,\mu\mathrm{V}$.
\end{enumerate}\begin{CornellSummaryBox}{Summary}
Refer noise to one point, integrate density through the real bandwidth, and choose devices from source impedance and frequency. Treat spectral lines as interference until proven otherwise, and validate the entire grounding, shielding, supply, and measurement setup.
\end{CornellSummaryBox}
