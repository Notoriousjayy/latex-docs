\begin{CornellOverviewBox}{Concept}
Precision design is controlled error allocation. Instead of choosing a part labeled `precision,' translate the measurement objective into an error budget covering resistors, references, amplifier inputs and outputs, common-mode effects, drift, noise, settling, leakage, thermal gradients, and calibration. Difference, instrumentation, and fully differential amplifiers extend the method to difficult source and converter interfaces.
\end{CornellOverviewBox}
\begin{CornellOverviewBox}{Concept}
\textbf{Prerequisites.} Op-amp feedback, noise gain, RC settling, decibels, resistor networks, and basic statistics for independent errors.

\textbf{After studying this chapter, you should be able to:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Construct worst-case and root-sum-square error budgets with input- or output-referred terms.
  \item Evaluate offset, drift, bias current, CMRR, PSRR, noise, gain error, distortion, and settling.
  \item Choose among precision, auto-zero, difference, instrumentation, and fully differential amplifiers.
  \item Design input protection and bias paths without silently destroying accuracy.
\end{itemize}\textbf{Central design questions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item What accuracy, resolution, bandwidth, temperature range, and settling time are actually required?
  \item Which errors are systematic, random, calibratable, or time/temperature dependent?
  \item Where will leakage, thermoelectric voltage, common-mode conversion, and layout defeat the schematic?
\end{itemize}\end{CornellOverviewBox}
\section{Cornell Notes}
\begin{CornellNotesTable}
\CornellNoteRow{5.1--5.5: Error-budget method}{Define the measurand and allowable total error before choosing components. Refer every term to the same node and state whether limits are worst-case, typical, or statistical. Worst-case sums magnitudes; independent random rms terms combine by root-sum-square. Include initial error, drift, noise over bandwidth, nonlinearity, loading, quantization, reference, settling, and calibration residual. Leave margin for unknowns.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.6: Component errors}{Resistors contribute ratio tolerance, absolute tolerance where impedance matters, temperature coefficient, voltage coefficient, excess noise, self-heating, and long-term drift. Capacitors add leakage, dielectric absorption, temperature/voltage coefficients, and soakage. Switches add leakage, charge injection, on-resistance, and thermoelectric offsets. Matched networks often outperform individually precise parts because ratio tracking dominates.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.7: Amplifier input errors}{Input impedance loads the source. Bias current times source resistance creates offset; current noise adds frequency-dependent noise. Input offset and drift are amplified by dc noise gain. Finite CMRR converts common-mode voltage to input-referred differential error; finite PSRR converts supply change. Protect inputs with balanced impedance and low-leakage components while preserving a bias-current return.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.8: Output and dynamic errors}{Slew rate bounds large-signal slope; GBW and loop gain control small-signal settling; output impedance, crossover, load current, and capacitive load affect accuracy. Settling includes linear exponential decay plus nonlinear recovery from slew or overload. Gain error and nonlinearity are distinct: calibration can remove constant gain but not curvature, hysteresis, or time-varying error.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.9: RRIO amplifiers}{Rail-to-rail input stages may hand off between transistor pairs, producing offset, bias, noise, or distortion discontinuities. Output swing depends on load current and direction. A part can be rail-to-rail at dc yet exhibit poor recovery or distortion near a rail. Keep the operating common-mode region away from crossover when precision matters, or verify behavior empirically.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.10: Precision selection}{Filter candidates by supply, input common-mode, output swing, source resistance, offset/drift, voltage and current noise, bias current, CMRR, PSRR, GBW, slew, settling, distortion, package, and temperature grade. Typical plots guide understanding; min/max tables support guarantees. No single amplifier simultaneously minimizes noise, bias, speed, power, and offset.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.11: Auto-zero amplifiers}{Auto-zero and chopper techniques reduce offset and low-frequency drift, but switching introduces ripple, aliasing, input-current transients, charge injection, and spectral artifacts. They excel for low-frequency, high-gain dc measurement when source impedance and filtering are compatible. Check noise spectrum beyond the advertised near-dc region and avoid unfiltered sampling interactions.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.12: High-resolution measurement}{Precision instruments succeed through architecture: guarded high-impedance inputs, staged gain, auto-ranging, stable references, multislope or integrating conversion, calibration storage, thermal design, shielding, and time-domain rejection of interference. Component accuracy alone is insufficient; the signal path, switching sequence, reference path, and calibration model form one measurement system.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.13--5.14: Difference amplifiers}{A four-resistor difference amplifier rejects common-mode voltage only when resistor ratios match. Source impedance unbalances ratios and degrades CMRR. Integrated matched networks improve tracking. Verify input common-mode range at the internal op-amp inputs and output range after common-mode plus differential conversion. Input protection must be symmetric.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.15--5.16: Instrumentation amplifiers}{Instrumentation amplifiers offer high input impedance and gain set without sacrificing input symmetry. The classic three-amplifier topology separates input buffering from subtraction. Check common-mode range versus gain and output, reference-pin drive, input bias return, source imbalance, EMI rectification, overload recovery, and protection current. `Rail-to-rail' claims do not remove the internal common-mode constraint.}
\CornellNoteRow{5.17: Fully differential amplifiers}{A fully differential amplifier controls both output difference and output common-mode, often to drive a differential ADC. Design includes differential gain, common-mode feedback, source/load termination, output common-mode target, anti-alias filtering, kickback isolation, balance, and stability. Common-mode output bandwidth and the ADC's dynamic input are part of the loop environment.}
\end{CornellNotesTable}
\begin{CornellSummaryBox}{Summary}
Precision is a system property demonstrated by a traceable error budget and validation plan. Static accuracy, noise, dynamic settling, common-mode behavior, thermal effects, leakage, protection, and calibration interact. Matched ratios and symmetry often matter more than absolute values. Specialized amplifiers solve particular error classes but introduce new constraints that must be budgeted rather than assumed away.
\end{CornellSummaryBox}
\section{Worked Examples}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Input-referred dc error budget}
A gain-20 stage has input offset limit $35\,\mu\mathrm{V}$, bias-current limit $2\,\mathrm{nA}$ with $12\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ source resistance, and resistor-ratio error equivalent to $18\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ input. Bias error is $24\,\mu\mathrm{V}$. Worst-case input error is $35+24+18=77\,\mu\mathrm{V}$, or $1.54\,\mathrm{mV}$ output. RSS is $46.2\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ input only if independence and statistical interpretation are justified.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{CMRR requirement}
A sensor produces $12\,\mathrm{mV}$ differential on $8\,\mathrm{V}$ common mode. To keep common-mode conversion below $6\,\mu\mathrm{V}$ input, required CMRR is $20\log_{10}(8/6\,\mu)=122.5\,\mathrm{dB}$. This severe requirement includes source imbalance and resistor network, not only the amplifier datasheet number.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Settling estimate}
A first-order closed-loop response with time constant $4\,\mu\mathrm{s}$ must settle to $0.01\%$. Linear settling time is $t=-\tau\ln(10^{-4})=36.8\,\mu\mathrm{s}$. Add time for slew and overload recovery; a small-signal exponential estimate is insufficient after a large step.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\section{Design and Troubleshooting}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Design and Troubleshooting}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Write a numerical measurement specification: range, accuracy, resolution, bandwidth, settling, environment, and calibration interval.
  \item Build an input-referred budget separating initial, drift, noise, dynamic, common-mode, reference, and calibration errors.
  \item Select architecture and parts from guaranteed limits; design symmetry, protection, guarding, filtering, and thermal layout together.
  \item Validate with zero/full-scale tests, common-mode and supply sweeps, temperature soak, noise spectra, step settling, overload recovery, and uncertainty records.
\end{enumerate}\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Symptoms, likely causes, and checks}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Offset changes when touched: thermal gradients, thermoelectric junctions, leakage, contamination, or unguarded high impedance.
  \item Error depends on common-mode voltage: resistor-ratio mismatch, source imbalance, limited CMRR, or input range violation.
  \item Good dc but poor repeated readings: noise bandwidth, auto-zero ripple, reference instability, dielectric absorption, or insufficient settling.
  \item Calibration fails across range: nonlinearity, self-heating, input loading, gain-dependent common-mode limits, or quantization.
  \item ADC drive distortion: inadequate settling, charge kickback, output-current limit, common-mode loop, or anti-alias filter interaction.
\end{itemize}\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellWarningBox}{Safety and Limitations}
Precision front ends connected to external sensors need fault-current limiting, overvoltage protection, and a defined path when unpowered. Protection devices can leak or add capacitance, so validate them at temperature without removing necessary safety. High common-mode voltage may exceed absolute maximum ratings even when the differential signal is tiny.
\end{CornellWarningBox}
\section{Key-Equation Sheet}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Key Equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Worst-case: $E_{WC}=\sum_i |e_i|$; independent rms estimate: $E_{RSS}=\sqrt{\sum_i e_i^2}$.
  \item Bias-current error: $V_{IB}=I_B R_S$; use source resistance seen by each input.
  \item Common-mode error estimate: $V_{err,in}=V_{CM}/10^{CMRR_{dB}/20}$.
  \item Integrated white-noise rms: $v_n=e_n\sqrt{B_n}$ for flat density $e_n$ and equivalent noise bandwidth $B_n$.
  \item First-order settling: $t=-\tau\ln(\epsilon)$ for fractional error $\epsilon$ after linear behavior begins.
  \item Resistor self-heating: $P=V^2/R=I^2R$; temperature rise times TCR creates a signal-dependent gain error.
\end{itemize}\end{CornellExamBox}
\section{Study and Review}
\subsection{Design checklist}
\begin{itemize}
  \item All errors share one reference plane and confidence convention.
  \item Guaranteed limits, temperature range, and calibration assumptions are explicit.
  \item Source impedance, bias path, CMRR, PSRR, leakage, and protection are included.
  \item Noise bandwidth, settling, overload recovery, and distortion meet the measurement timing.
  \item Layout controls thermal gradients, contamination, guarding, grounding, and reference return.
\end{itemize}\subsection{Common misconceptions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item More display digits do not imply greater accuracy.
  \item A low offset amplifier can still be poor because of bias current, noise, drift, or CMRR.
  \item Calibration does not remove noise, nonlinearity, hysteresis, or future drift.
  \item Integrated resistor matching can matter more than low absolute tolerance.
  \item Auto-zero amplifiers are not free of switching artifacts.
\end{itemize}\subsection{Active-recall questions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item When is RSS combination justified?
  \item Why refer errors to one node?
  \item How does source resistance affect bias and current-noise error?
  \item What degrades a difference amplifier's CMRR?
  \item Why can rail-to-rail input crossover harm precision?
  \item What is dielectric absorption?
  \item Which errors can one-point and two-point calibration remove?
  \item How do slew and linear settling differ?
  \item Why does a differential ADC need common-mode control?
  \item What tests expose thermoelectric or leakage errors?
\end{enumerate}\subsection{Original practice problems}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Combine independent rms errors of $8$, $12$, and $20\,\mu\mathrm{V}$.
  \item A $5\,\mathrm{V}$ common-mode signal enters a stage with $100\,\mathrm{dB}$ CMRR. Estimate input-referred error.
  \item White voltage noise is $9\,\mathrm{nV}/\sqrt{\mathrm{Hz}}$ over $20\,\mathrm{kHz}$ equivalent bandwidth. Find rms noise.
\end{enumerate}\subsection{Answer key / solution outline}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $\sqrt{8^2+12^2+20^2}=24.7\,\mu\mathrm{V}$; worst-case would be $40\,\mu\mathrm{V}$.
  \item $5/10^{100/20}=50\,\mu\mathrm{V}$, before source/resistor imbalance.
  \item $9\sqrt{20000}=1.27\,\mu\mathrm{V}_{rms}$, excluding $1/f$, current, resistor, and interference terms.
\end{enumerate}\begin{CornellSummaryBox}{Summary}
Start with a numerical error budget, not a component label. Control ratios, symmetry, bandwidth, temperature, leakage, protection, settling, and calibration as one system; then verify the budget with tests that isolate each error mechanism.
\end{CornellSummaryBox}
