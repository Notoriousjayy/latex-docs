\begin{CornellOverviewBox}{Concept}
Operational amplifiers turn high open-loop gain into predictable closed-loop functions through negative feedback. Ideal rules make topology recognition fast, while real designs must budget input/output range, offset, bias current, noise, gain-bandwidth, slew rate, output drive, capacitive-load behavior, recovery, and loop stability.
\end{CornellOverviewBox}
\begin{CornellOverviewBox}{Concept}
\textbf{Prerequisites.} Resistive networks, complex impedance, RC filters, transistor gain stages, and the meaning of negative feedback.

\textbf{After studying this chapter, you should be able to:}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Derive gains and impedances for common inverting, noninverting, difference, current-source, and integrator circuits.
  \item Apply ideal op-amp rules only after confirming negative feedback and linear operation.
  \item Translate datasheet limitations into dc, transient, noise, and stability error terms.
  \item Design single-supply signal paths and compensate feedback networks.
\end{itemize}\textbf{Central design questions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Can the chosen amplifier keep both inputs and output inside valid ranges?
  \item At what frequency does loop gain cease to make the ideal equations accurate?
  \item Which error source dominates: offset, bias, noise, bandwidth, slew, loading, or instability?
\end{itemize}\end{CornellOverviewBox}
\section{Cornell Notes}
\begin{CornellNotesTable}
\CornellNoteRow{4.1: Ideal model}{With negative feedback and linear operation, input currents are approximately zero and $V_+\approx V_-$. These are consequences of high open-loop gain, not universal laws. First identify the feedback path and expected output polarity; then solve the external network. If the calculated output exceeds a rail or rate limit, the ideal solution is invalid.}
\CornellNoteRow{4.2: Basic circuits}{Inverting gain is $-R_f/R_{in}$ and input impedance is approximately $R_{in}$. Noninverting gain is $1+R_f/R_g$ and input impedance is high. Followers buffer; difference amplifiers require ratio matching for common-mode rejection; current sources regulate a sensed voltage across a resistor; integrators accumulate input while practical dc feedback prevents saturation. Always provide an input-bias-current path.}
\CornellNoteRow{4.3: Linear and nonlinear functions}{Summers, active rectifiers, clamps, peak detectors, oscillators, pulse-width generators, and active filters combine op-amp feedback with diodes, capacitors, or switches. Analyze piecewise operating states and verify transition behavior. A nonlinear element inside feedback can reduce apparent junction error, but finite slew, saturation recovery, leakage, and bandwidth remain.}
\CornellNoteRow{4.4: Real op-amp behavior}{Key limits include input common-mode range, differential input tolerance, output swing versus load, output current, offset and drift, input bias/current noise, open-loop gain, CMRR, PSRR, gain-bandwidth, slew rate, distortion, and recovery. Rail-to-rail labels are conditional. Source impedance converts bias current to voltage error, and closed-loop noise gain determines bandwidth and stability.}
\CornellNoteRow{4.5: Selected precision functions}{Peak detectors and sample/hold circuits are limited by diode or switch charge, droop, dielectric absorption, acquisition time, and amplifier recovery. Active clamps and absolute-value circuits require correct state transitions. Integrators and differentiators need bandwidth-limiting components so noise and offsets do not grow without bound.}
\CornellNoteRow{4.6: Single-supply operation}{Single-supply circuits often create a quiet midrail reference and express ac signals around it. The reference must sink/source expected current or be buffered. Check input common-mode range during startup and transients, output swing under both source and sink load, and how coupling capacitors establish low-frequency corners. Capacitive loads may require isolation resistance or a stable driver.}
\CornellNoteRow{4.7--4.8: Amplifier selection and applications}{General-purpose, precision, low-noise, high-speed, current-feedback, instrumentation, power, and high-voltage amplifiers optimize different constraints. Select from required supply, signal, source impedance, closed-loop gain, load, noise band, temperature, and package. A circuit that works with a generic symbol may fail with a real pinout, shutdown pin, exposed pad, or minimum stable gain.}
\CornellNoteRow{4.9: Frequency compensation}{Loop stability depends on magnitude and phase of loop gain $T=A\beta$. The loop should cross unity with adequate phase margin and controlled gain slope. Noise gain, not signal gain, sets stability for many configurations. Feedback capacitance, input capacitance, capacitive loads, and layout add poles/zeros. Compensation trades bandwidth or settling for margin; verify with step response and, when possible, loop analysis.}
\end{CornellNotesTable}
\begin{CornellSummaryBox}{Summary}
Op-amp design starts with ideal feedback equations and ends with proof that the real amplifier can realize them. Closed-loop topology sets signal gain and noise gain; datasheet ranges and dynamic limits decide validity. Single-supply bias, source impedance, capacitive loading, layout, and feedback compensation must be treated as part of the circuit, not as afterthoughts.
\end{CornellSummaryBox}
\section{Worked Examples}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Inverting stage and bandwidth}
Choose $R_{in}=10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ and $R_f=47\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$, giving $A_v=-4.7$ and noise gain $1+47/10=5.7$. With a $12\,\mathrm{MHz}$ gain-bandwidth amplifier, a first bandwidth estimate is $12/5.7=2.1\,\mathrm{MHz}$, not $12/4.7$. Parasitic capacitance and phase-margin requirements may reduce it.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Bias-current error}
A noninverting input sees $80\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$ source resistance and the input bias current is $25\,\mathrm{nA}$. The resulting input-referred error is $2.0\,\mathrm{mV}$. At noise gain 11, that becomes about $22\,\mathrm{mV}$ output error. Adding a matching resistance on the other input helps only when bias currents track; input-current difference and resistor noise remain.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Slew-rate requirement}
A $6\,\mathrm{V_{pk}}$, $100\,\mathrm{kHz}$ sine requires $SR_{min}=2\pi fV_p=3.77\,\mathrm{V/\mu s}$. Select appreciable margin over this value and check output load. If the amplifier supplies only $2\,\mathrm{V/\mu s}$, the waveform becomes slope-limited even if small-signal bandwidth appears sufficient.
\end{CornellExamBox}
\section{Design and Troubleshooting}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Design and Troubleshooting}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Define supply, input common-mode range, output swing/current, source impedance, gain, bandwidth, noise, and accuracy.
  \item Solve with the ideal model; compute noise gain, dc error gains, low/high-frequency corners, and required slew rate.
  \item Screen real candidates against guaranteed ranges and load conditions; add bias paths, decoupling, protection, and compensation.
  \item Prototype with clean grounding; measure dc operating points, noise, step response, overload recovery, and worst-case load behavior.
\end{enumerate}\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Symptoms, likely causes, and checks}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Output pinned at a rail: wrong feedback sign, invalid common-mode input, missing bias path, offset integration, or excessive demanded swing.
  \item Oscillation/ringing: capacitive load, high feedback impedance, poor bypassing, excessive source capacitance, or unsuitable noise gain.
  \item Unexpected dc error: input offset, bias current through source resistance, CMRR/PSRR, leakage, or thermoelectric junctions.
  \item Distorted large signal: slew limiting, output current limiting, crossover, rail recovery, or insufficient settling time.
  \item Noisy result: excessive resistor value, wide bandwidth, input-current noise, reference noise, or grounding interference.
\end{itemize}\end{CornellExamBox}
\begin{CornellWarningBox}{Safety and Limitations}
Op-amp inputs and outputs may be damaged by signals present when supplies are off or by voltages outside rails. Limit current with resistors or approved clamps, and check phase reversal and latch-up behavior. High-voltage and power op-amps require creepage, heatsinking, current limiting, and awareness that exposed packages or heatsinks may carry hazardous potential.
\end{CornellWarningBox}
\section{Key-Equation Sheet}
\begin{CornellExamBox}{Key Equations}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Inverting: $A_v=-R_f/R_{in}$; noise gain $=1+R_f/R_{in}$.
  \item Noninverting: $A_v=1+R_f/R_g$.
  \item Integrator: $v_o(t)=-\frac{1}{RC}\int v_i(t)\,dt$ in the frequency/time range where the ideal assumptions hold.
  \item Full-power sine requirement: $SR\ge2\pi fV_p$.
  \item First closed-loop bandwidth estimate: $f_{BW}\approx GBW/(\text{noise gain})$ for a single-pole voltage-feedback amplifier.
  \item Input bias error estimate: $V_{err}=I_B R_S$; propagate by the appropriate dc noise gain.
\end{itemize}\end{CornellExamBox}
\section{Study and Review}
\subsection{Design checklist}
\begin{itemize}
  \item Negative feedback and linear operating state are verified.
  \item Input common-mode and output swing pass at all signal and startup states.
  \item Noise gain, GBW, slew, settling, and phase margin are adequate.
  \item Offset, bias, resistor, CMRR, PSRR, and noise errors fit the budget.
  \item Supply bypassing, reference return, layout, load capacitance, and protection are defined.
\end{itemize}\subsection{Common misconceptions}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The inputs are not literally equal; feedback makes their difference small while linear.
  \item Rail-to-rail does not mean zero headroom at every load and temperature.
  \item Gain-bandwidth divided by signal gain is not always the correct bandwidth estimate; use noise gain.
  \item A stable dc output does not prove adequate phase margin.
  \item An input bias current path is required even for ac-coupled signals.
\end{itemize}\subsection{Active-recall questions}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item State the two ideal op-amp rules and their conditions.
  \item What is noise gain?
  \item Why can a follower oscillate with a capacitive load?
  \item How does source resistance convert bias current to error?
  \item What limits an active peak detector?
  \item How is single-supply midrail generated and loaded?
  \item What is the difference between GBW and slew rate?
  \item Why does difference-amplifier CMRR depend on resistor matching?
  \item What does phase margin predict qualitatively?
  \item Which measurements reveal poor stability?
\end{enumerate}\subsection{Original practice problems}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Design a noninverting gain of 8 using $R_g=10\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$.
  \item A $5\,\mathrm{MHz}$ GBW amplifier is used at noise gain 25. Estimate bandwidth.
  \item Find minimum slew rate for $2.5\,\mathrm{V_{pk}}$ at $400\,\mathrm{kHz}$.
\end{enumerate}\subsection{Answer key / solution outline}
\begin{enumerate}
  \item $R_f=(8-1)R_g=70\,\mathrm{k\Omega}$; choose a standard/precision value and include tolerance in the gain error.
  \item About $200\,\mathrm{kHz}$ for a dominant-pole approximation; verify phase margin and settling in the datasheet.
  \item $2\pi(400\,\mathrm{kHz})(2.5\,\mathrm{V})=6.28\,\mathrm{V/\mu s}$; select margin.
\end{enumerate}\begin{CornellSummaryBox}{Summary}
Solve the feedback network ideally, then prove the amplifier remains linear, fast enough, quiet enough, stable, and within every input/output and thermal limit. Noise gain is central to both bandwidth and stability.
\end{CornellSummaryBox}
