\section*{\color{navy}Domain Overview}
Domain 1 establishes the governance, ethics, legal, risk, continuity, personnel, supply-chain, and awareness foundations on which the remaining CISSP domains rely. The CISSP perspective is management-led and risk-based: protect confidentiality, integrity, and availability in a way that supports organizational objectives, complies with obligations, and keeps residual risk within leadership's tolerance.

\begin{summarybox}{Review Objectives}
After studying these notes, you should be able to explain professional ethics and the CIA triad; connect security governance to business strategy; distinguish legal, regulatory, contractual, and privacy obligations; structure policy and continuity planning; calculate and treat risk; select and assess controls; apply threat-modeling approaches; manage supply-chain risk; and evaluate an awareness, education, and training program.
\end{summarybox}

\section{\color{navy}Professional Ethics and Core Security Concepts}
\source{pp. 2--7}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Why begin with professional ethics?}{Security practitioners are entrusted with sensitive information, privileged capabilities, and decisions that affect people and organizations. Ethical conduct constrains how that trust is exercised even when a technically possible action is not appropriate.}
\noterow{(ISC)\textsuperscript{2} Code of Ethics}{Certified professionals agree to the Code and its canons. The CBK emphasizes protecting society and public trust, acting honorably and legally, serving principals diligently, and advancing the profession. When duties appear to conflict, higher-order obligations to society and ethical practice guide judgment.}
\noterow{Organizational codes of ethics}{An employer's code translates values into expected workplace behavior. It complements professional obligations but does not excuse illegal or unethical conduct. Security professionals should understand both sets of expectations and escalate conflicts appropriately.}
\noterow{What is confidentiality?}{Prevent unauthorized disclosure of information. Typical safeguards include access control, encryption, data classification, need-to-know, and physical protection. A confidentiality failure exposes information to a party that is not authorized to receive it.}
\noterow{What is integrity?}{Preserve accuracy, completeness, and authorized state. Hashing, digital signatures, change control, access control, backups, and validation can prevent or reveal improper modification. Authenticity and nonrepudiation are closely related to integrity.}
\noterow{What is availability?}{Ensure authorized users can obtain information and services when needed. Redundancy, fault tolerance, capacity planning, resilient architecture, backups, disaster recovery, and protection from denial-of-service conditions support availability.}
\noterow{Why is the CIA triad insufficient by itself?}{CIA is a foundational model, not a complete security program. Real decisions also consider authenticity, accountability, nonrepudiation, privacy, safety, utility, possession/control, business objectives, law, and risk. Use CIA as a lens rather than a complete checklist.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Security Governance Principles}
\source{pp. 8--22}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{What is security governance?}{The structures and decision processes through which leadership directs and oversees information security. Governance connects security strategy, policy, resources, accountability, and risk decisions to the organization's mission and objectives.}
\noterow{Business alignment}{Security exists to enable the organization, not as an isolated technical function. Strategy should begin with mission, goals, risk appetite/tolerance, legal duties, and business processes, then establish security objectives and investments that support them.}
\noterow{Organizational processes: acquisitions and mergers}{Security due diligence should examine the target's policies, regulated data, personnel practices, proprietary applications, penetration-test results, third-party/open-source dependencies, and gaps before networks and data are integrated. An acquisition can import unknown vulnerabilities and new attack paths.}
\noterow{Organizational processes: divestitures}{Inventory the assets and information leaving the organization, decouple shared systems, review and revoke access as roles change, and meet retention/deletion obligations. Confidential data must not leave unintentionally with hardware, accounts, or personnel.}
\noterow{Who owns security?}{Senior leadership sets direction and accepts organizational risk. The CISO leads the information security program; managers own processes; analysts execute operational work. Asset/data owners make protection decisions, while every user has responsibilities for policy compliance, training, and reporting.}
\noterow{Top-down vs. bottom-up security}{A top-down program begins with leadership, business requirements, policy, and resources; the CBK favors this governance model. Bottom-up efforts can provide technical improvements but often lack authority, funding, and enterprise alignment.}
\noterow{What is a security control framework?}{A structured set of practices and controls used to organize security and demonstrate that risk is managed. Selection depends on industry, jurisdiction, contractual obligations, and business needs; frameworks can be tailored and combined.}
\noterow{ISO/IEC 27001 and 27002}{ISO/IEC 27001 specifies requirements for establishing and maintaining an information security management system (ISMS). ISO/IEC 27002 provides a catalog of security control guidance. Together they support a governed, risk-based security program.}
\noterow{NIST and CIS frameworks}{NIST publications such as SP 800-53 and the NIST Cybersecurity Framework provide widely used control and program guidance. CIS Critical Security Controls provide prioritized defensive practices. The correct choice follows organizational requirements rather than brand preference.}
\noterow{COBIT, PCI, HIPAA, HITRUST}{COBIT focuses broadly on IT governance and management. PCI DSS addresses payment-card environments. HIPAA establishes U.S. healthcare-related obligations, while HITRUST provides an auditable control framework designed to harmonize overlapping requirements. Know the scope and purpose before mapping controls.}
\noterow{Due care vs. due diligence}{\textbf{Due care} is taking the reasonable protective actions expected of a prudent organization. \textbf{Due diligence} is the ongoing investigation, evaluation, and verification used to understand risk and ensure those actions remain appropriate. A mature program requires both.}
\end{cornell}

\begin{exambox}{Key Distinction}
Governance sets direction and accountability; management implements that direction. Risk acceptance is a business/leadership decision informed by security expertise, not a unilateral technical-team decision.
\end{exambox}

\section{\color{navy}Compliance, Law, Regulation, and Privacy}
\source{pp. 23--54}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{How should requirements be determined?}{Identify every jurisdiction in which the organization operates or handles data, then map statutes, regulations, industry standards, contracts, privacy commitments, and internal policy. Applicability depends on facts such as sector, data type, location, and business relationship.}
\noterow{Legislative vs. regulatory requirements}{Legislatures create laws; authorized agencies issue and enforce regulations within their mandate. Security programs must translate applicable obligations into controls and evidence rather than treating compliance as a one-time project.}
\noterow{Industry and contractual obligations}{Standards or contracts may impose requirements even when they are not statutes. PCI DSS is a prominent payment-card example. Service agreements, customer terms, licensing terms, and partner contracts can also create enforceable security duties.}
\noterow{Privacy requirements}{Privacy addresses appropriate collection, use, processing, disclosure, retention, and protection of information about people. Requirements vary by jurisdiction. The organization should know what personal data it holds, why it holds it, where it flows, and which rights or duties apply.}
\noterow{Cybercrime and data-breach law}{Computer misuse, unauthorized access, fraud, interception, and breach notification may be governed by multiple laws. Jurisdiction can be complex because actors, systems, victims, and data may reside in different places. Coordinate security response with legal counsel and law enforcement when appropriate.}
\noterow{U.S. legal landscape -- study cue}{The chapter surveys major U.S. statutes affecting computer crime, electronic communications, government systems, privacy, and sector-specific data. Focus on each law's purpose and scope rather than memorizing a statute as a universal rule; applicability depends on the organization and data involved.}
\noterow{Intellectual property categories}{\textbf{Copyright} protects original expression; \textbf{patents} protect qualifying inventions; \textbf{trademarks} identify sources/brands; \textbf{trade secrets} protect valuable confidential business information when secrecy is maintained. Security controls help preserve these rights.}
\noterow{Software licensing}{A license defines permitted use, copying, distribution, modification, and related obligations. Organizations should inventory software and dependencies, respect proprietary/open-source terms, and avoid unauthorized copying or deployment.}
\noterow{Import/export controls}{Some technologies and cryptographic products may be subject to national import/export restrictions. Security teams should involve legal/compliance specialists before transferring controlled technology, source code, hardware, or technical information across borders.}
\noterow{Transborder data flow}{Moving or allowing access to data across national boundaries can trigger multiple jurisdictions' privacy and security rules. Data location, processing location, contractual mechanisms, and onward transfers all matter.}
\noterow{Privacy governance}{Treat privacy as a lifecycle concern. Define lawful/approved purposes; minimize unnecessary collection; protect data; respect notice/choice and individual rights where applicable; control disclosure; retain only as required; and dispose securely.}
\noterow{Administrative investigations}{Internal organizational inquiries generally address policy or employment matters. The organization should follow its policies, preserve evidence, respect employee rights and privacy, and coordinate with HR/legal functions.}
\noterow{Criminal investigations}{Government authorities investigate alleged crimes under criminal law. Evidence standards and constitutional/statutory protections can be strict; organizational responders should preserve evidence and avoid actions that damage admissibility or interfere with law enforcement.}
\noterow{Civil investigations}{Civil matters resolve disputes between parties and may result in damages or injunctions. Discovery can require production of electronically stored information, making preservation, legal hold, records management, and defensible evidence handling important.}
\noterow{Regulatory and industry investigations}{Regulators assess compliance with laws/rules within their jurisdiction; industry bodies or contractual assessors evaluate conformance to standards such as PCI. Required authority, evidence, reporting, and consequences differ from criminal or civil cases.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Policy, Continuity, and Personnel Security}
\source{pp. 55--68}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Policy vs. standard vs. procedure vs. guideline}{\textbf{Policy}: management's high-level mandatory direction. \textbf{Standard}: mandatory specific requirement supporting policy. \textbf{Procedure}: required step-by-step method for a task. \textbf{Guideline}: recommended practice offering flexibility. Keep lower-level documents consistent with policy.}
\noterow{Why use a document hierarchy?}{A hierarchy separates stable management intent from implementation details that change more frequently. It also makes accountability, training, audit evidence, exception handling, and maintenance easier.}
\noterow{Business continuity planning (BCP)}{BCP prepares the organization to sustain or restore critical business functions through disruptive events. Security contributes risk information and control expertise, but continuity is a business-wide responsibility with executive sponsorship.}
\noterow{Business impact analysis (BIA)}{Identify critical processes and dependencies, evaluate impacts of interruption, and establish recovery priorities and requirements. Consider financial, operational, legal/regulatory, safety, and reputational consequences, including how impacts change over time.}
\noterow{BCP scope and plan}{Define objectives, scope, assumptions, stakeholders, critical functions, dependencies, recovery requirements, roles, communications, and maintenance/testing expectations. Management approval and recurring review keep the plan aligned with business change.}
\noterow{Candidate screening and hiring}{Personnel security begins before access is granted. Apply lawful, role-appropriate screening; verify qualifications where appropriate; define responsibilities; and use least privilege so hiring does not automatically confer unnecessary access.}
\noterow{Employment agreements and policies}{Use acceptable-use, confidentiality, nondisclosure, intellectual-property, and other agreements/policies appropriate to the role and jurisdiction. Ensure personnel understand obligations and consequences.}
\noterow{Onboarding, transfer, termination}{Provision only approved access at onboarding; promptly adjust access when responsibilities change; and revoke access, recover assets, preserve necessary records, and address continuing confidentiality duties at termination.}
\noterow{Vendors, consultants, contractors}{Third parties require defined security expectations, appropriate screening and access, contractual controls, monitoring, and timely deprovisioning. Their different employment relationship does not reduce risk.}
\noterow{Compliance and privacy in personnel policy}{Personnel controls must satisfy employment law, contractual obligations, privacy requirements, and the organization's own policies. Collect and retain employee information only as needed and protect it appropriately.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Risk Management}
\source{pp. 68--83}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Threat, vulnerability, risk}{A \textbf{threat} is a potential cause of harm; a \textbf{vulnerability} is a weakness that can be exploited; \textbf{risk} reflects the possibility and consequence of a threat exploiting a vulnerability. Controls change likelihood and/or impact but rarely eliminate all risk.}
\noterow{Risk assessment flow}{Establish context and assets, identify threats and vulnerabilities, analyze likelihood and impact, determine risk, compare it with tolerance/criteria, prioritize, and communicate results. Reassess as the environment changes.}
\noterow{Qualitative vs. quantitative analysis}{Qualitative analysis uses relative scales and expert judgment (for example low/medium/high). Quantitative analysis expresses frequency and impact numerically, often in money. Each has uncertainty; choose a method that enables consistent, decision-useful prioritization.}
\noterow{Formula: Single Loss Expectancy}{\[
\mathrm{SLE}=\mathrm{AV}\times\mathrm{EF}
\]
AV is asset value; EF is the proportion of value expected to be lost in one event. Example: a \$200,000 asset with a 25\% exposure has SLE = \$50,000.}
\noterow{Formula: Annualized Loss Expectancy}{\[
\mathrm{ALE}=\mathrm{SLE}\times\mathrm{ARO}
\]
ARO is expected occurrences per year. If SLE is \$50,000 and ARO is 0.2, ALE is \$10,000/year. The estimate supports comparison of risk-reduction options; it is not a guarantee of loss.}
\noterow{Risk evaluation and tolerance}{Compare assessed risk with leadership's risk appetite/tolerance and established criteria. The result drives prioritization and treatment. Residual risk after controls must be understood and accepted by the appropriate authority.}
\noterow{Four risk responses}{\textbf{Avoid}: stop the risky activity. \textbf{Mitigate}: reduce likelihood/impact with controls. \textbf{Transfer}: shift/share financial consequences or responsibility through mechanisms such as insurance/contracts. \textbf{Accept}: knowingly retain risk within tolerance.}
\noterow{Selecting countermeasures}{Consider effectiveness, feasibility, regulatory/contractual needs, lifecycle cost, new risks, and business impact. The CBK's economic test is that the protected value/risk reduction should justify the control cost.}
\noterow{Countermeasure cost reasoning}{A control is economically sensible when the remaining ALE plus the annualized control cost is less than the untreated ALE, subject to mandatory requirements and nonfinancial impacts. Risk decisions should not reduce everything to dollars when safety, law, or reputation dominates.}
\noterow{Control functions}{\textbf{Preventive} controls try to stop events; \textbf{detective} controls identify events; \textbf{corrective} controls restore/repair; \textbf{deterrent} controls discourage; \textbf{compensating} controls provide alternative protection; \textbf{recovery} controls restore capability. A defense-in-depth design combines functions.}
\noterow{Control categories}{Controls are commonly characterized as technical/logical, operational, and management/administrative. Classification helps coverage analysis, but a control may span categories; focus on the actual risk-reduction objective.}
\noterow{Control assessment}{Verify that controls are implemented correctly, operating as intended, and achieving desired outcomes. Assessment evidence can include testing, observation, interviews, configuration review, metrics, audit results, and logs.}
\noterow{Monitoring, measurement, reporting}{Monitor risk indicators, control performance, threats, vulnerabilities, incidents, exceptions, and changes. Reports should be tailored to decision makers and show trends, exposure, ownership, and actions rather than raw technical data alone.}
\noterow{Continuous improvement}{Risk management is cyclical. Lessons from incidents, audits, assessments, metrics, business change, and new threats should update risk analysis, controls, documentation, and priorities.}
\noterow{ISO 31000}{A general risk-management framework intended for organizations of any type. Its principles emphasize an integrated, structured, customized, inclusive, dynamic approach using the best available information, accounting for human/cultural factors, and continually improving.}
\noterow{ISO/IEC 27005}{Provides information-security risk-management guidance and context, including tolerance, compliance expectations, treatment, communication, and monitoring. It can support qualitative or quantitative assessment if applied consistently.}
\noterow{NIST Risk Management Framework}{The chapter presents the six-step RMF cycle from NIST SP 800-37: \textbf{Categorize} systems, \textbf{Select} controls, \textbf{Implement} controls, \textbf{Assess} controls, \textbf{Authorize} the system, and continuously \textbf{Monitor} controls. Authorization is the leadership risk decision.}
\end{cornell}

\begin{exambox}{Management Perspective}
Risk treatment is not synonymous with installing a control. The sequence is: understand business context and risk, select a response, implement appropriate controls, assess effectiveness, communicate residual risk, obtain appropriate authorization/acceptance, and monitor continuously.
\end{exambox}

\section{\color{navy}Threat Modeling}
\source{pp. 83--87}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Why threat model?}{Threat modeling systematically identifies what must be protected, likely adversarial actions, weaknesses/attack paths, and appropriate mitigations. It shifts security analysis earlier in design and makes assumptions explicit.}
\noterow{STRIDE}{Microsoft mnemonic for six threat classes: \textbf{S}poofing, \textbf{T}ampering, \textbf{R}epudiation, \textbf{I}nformation disclosure, \textbf{D}enial of service, and \textbf{E}levation of privilege. These map naturally to authentication, integrity, nonrepudiation/accountability, confidentiality, availability, and authorization concerns.}
\noterow{PASTA}{Process for Attack Simulation and Threat Analysis is risk-based and connects business objectives with technical analysis. Seven stages: define objectives; define technical scope; application decomposition; threat analysis; vulnerability analysis; attack enumeration; risk and impact analysis.}
\noterow{NIST SP 800-154}{Data-centric threat-modeling approach: identify and characterize the system/data; select attack vectors; characterize controls that mitigate them; analyze the resulting threat model. It emphasizes protection tailored to the sensitive data rather than generic best practice alone.}
\noterow{DREAD}{Legacy quantitative-rating mnemonic: \textbf{D}amage, \textbf{R}eproducibility, \textbf{E}xploitability, \textbf{A}ffected users, \textbf{D}iscoverability. The chapter notes that Microsoft abandoned it, but the mnemonic remains useful to recognize.}
\noterow{Other models}{OCTAVE focuses on organizational information-security risk; Trike uses threat modeling as a risk-management technique; CORAS uses UML-oriented visualization; VAST applies an agile, scalable approach. Choose a method that matches organizational process and decision needs.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Supply-Chain Risk Management}
\source{pp. 88--91}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Why is supply-chain risk distinct?}{Hardware, software, services, components, integrators, and upstream dependencies can introduce vulnerabilities, malicious modification, counterfeit components, availability failures, and legal/licensing exposure outside the organization's direct control.}
\noterow{Third-party assessment and monitoring}{Perform risk-based due diligence before engagement and continue monitoring after contracting. Evidence may include questionnaires, audits, certifications/attestations, vulnerability information, penetration-test results, incident history, and contract performance.}
\noterow{Minimum security requirements (MSRs)}{Define the lowest acceptable security baseline a supplier must satisfy. Requirements should reflect data/system sensitivity and be enforceable through procurement and contracts rather than discovered after integration.}
\noterow{Service-level requirements}{SLAs translate service expectations into measurable commitments such as availability, response, recovery, notification, or support. Security requirements need owners, metrics, reporting, remedies, and exit/termination provisions.}
\noterow{Supply-chain frameworks}{The chapter highlights NIST supply-chain guidance and ISO 28000. NIST practices emphasize identifying supply-chain elements/actors, limiting access, maintaining provenance, controlled information sharing, training, defensive design, continuous review, and secure lifecycle/disposal practices.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}Security Awareness, Education, and Training}
\source{pp. 92--95}
\begin{cornell}
\noterow{Awareness vs. training vs. education}{\textbf{Awareness} keeps security visible and influences behavior; \textbf{training} builds skills for tasks or roles; \textbf{education} develops deeper knowledge and professional capability. A mature program uses the appropriate method for the audience and objective.}
\noterow{Delivery methods}{Use a mix of initial and recurring training, role-based instruction, brief awareness messages, simulations/exercises, posters/communications, and targeted coaching. Content should be accessible and relevant to actual threats and responsibilities.}
\noterow{Periodic content review}{Update material for policy changes, new technologies, emerging threats, incidents, regulatory changes, and audience feedback. Stale annual material is not evidence of an effective program.}
\noterow{How is effectiveness evaluated?}{Measure outcomes, not attendance alone. Possible indicators include assessment results, simulation performance, incident/reporting trends, policy violations, completion rates, help-desk/security reports, and demonstrated behavior change. Use results to improve content.}
\end{cornell}

\section{\color{navy}High-Yield Review}
\begin{itemize}
\item Security governance begins with business objectives and leadership accountability.
\item CIA is foundational, but sound security decisions also require risk, privacy, authenticity, accountability, law, and business context.
\item Know the policy hierarchy and distinguish due care from due diligence.
\item Quantitative risk: \(SLE=AV\times EF\) and \(ALE=SLE\times ARO\).
\item Risk responses are avoid, mitigate, transfer, and accept; leadership owns acceptance.
\item NIST RMF in this edition: categorize, select, implement, assess, authorize, monitor.
\item Threat modeling turns threats into structured design/risk decisions; know STRIDE, PASTA, NIST SP 800-154, and DREAD.
\item Third-party security requires pre-contract requirements plus continuous assessment and monitoring.
\item Awareness programs are judged by changed behavior and reduced risk, not completion counts alone.
\end{itemize}

\end{enumerate}

\section{\color{navy}Cornell Summary}
\begin{summarybox}{Domain 1 Summary}
Security and risk management is the discipline of translating organizational purpose and obligations into governed, ethical, risk-based protection. Leadership establishes direction, tolerance, policy, and accountability; practitioners identify threats and vulnerabilities, analyze risk, select and assess controls, and communicate residual exposure. Legal, privacy, personnel, continuity, supply-chain, and awareness responsibilities are part of the same system of governance. The CISSP mindset is therefore to choose defensible actions that protect the organization while serving its mission -- not merely to deploy the most technical control.
\end{summarybox}
